=== 00_hook ===
For centuries, these cities were gone. Buried under ash. Swallowed by jungle. Hidden beneath the sand. Some were written off as legends. Then someone found them, often by accident. A storm. A search for stray cattle. A man knocking down a wall in his own house. These are ten lost cities that were found again. And at the end, the one lost city nobody has ever found.

=== 01_skara_brae ===
Number ten. [Skara](/skˈɑɹə/) Brae, Scotland.

In eighteen fifty, a violent storm battered the [Orkney](/ˈɔɹkni/) Islands, off the north coast of Scotland. When it passed, the wind had stripped the sand from a large dune by the sea. Underneath were stone walls. Whole houses, linked by low passages, still standing after thousands of years.

[Skara](/skˈɑɹə/) Brae is nearly five thousand years old. That makes it older than the Egyptian pyramids, and older than Stonehenge. Inside the houses, the furniture is still there, because it was made of stone: beds, a hearth in the middle of the floor, and watertight boxes set into the ground, possibly for keeping fishing bait. Around two thousand five hundred B C, the people of [Skara](/skˈɑɹə/) Brae slowly abandoned their village. Nobody knows exactly why. The sand covered it, and kept it safe, until the storm brought it back.

=== 02_cliff_palace ===
Number nine. Cliff Palace, Colorado, USA.

On December eighteenth, eighteen eighty-eight, two cowboys, Richard [Wetherill](/wˈɛðəɹɪl/) and his brother-in-law Charlie Mason, were riding across Mesa [Verde](/vˈɜɹdi/) in southwestern Colorado, looking for lost cattle. From the rim of a canyon, they spotted something that should not have been there: an entire town tucked under the overhang of a cliff. Towers, walls and windows, silent and empty.

They had found Cliff Palace. Ancestral [Puebloan](/pwˈɛblOən/) people built it around eight hundred years ago, fitting it into a natural alcove in the sandstone. It has about one hundred and fifty rooms and twenty-three kivas, round sunken rooms used for ceremonies, and around one hundred people lived here. But by about thirteen hundred, its builders had moved away to the south, and the town stood empty for nearly six hundred years, until two men looking for their cows rode by.

=== 03_derinkuyu ===
Number eight. [Derinkuyu](/dˌɛɹɪnkˈuju/), Turkey.

In nineteen sixty-three, a man in the town of [Derinkuyu](/dˌɛɹɪnkˈuju/), in central Turkey, was renovating his house. He knocked down a wall in his basement and found a room behind it. Behind that room was another one. And then another. He had stumbled into the entrance of an entire city, carved into the soft volcanic rock beneath his feet.

[Derinkuyu](/dˌɛɹɪnkˈuju/) goes down about eighty-five meters and has at least eighteen levels. It could shelter up to twenty thousand people, along with their animals and supplies. There are stables, cellars, wells, schools and chapels, all connected by tunnels. More than fifty air shafts kept the air fresh, and huge stone doors could be rolled across the passages to seal them from the inside. Parts of it may date back to the eighth century B C, and people were still using some of its rooms in the nineteenth century.

=== 04_mohenjo_daro ===
Number seven. [Mohenjo](/mOhˈɛnʤO/) [daro](/dˈɑɹO/), Pakistan.

In the nineteenth century, British engineers building the railway between Lahore and Multan needed ballast for the track. Near the village of Harappa, they found a huge mound full of old, perfectly baked bricks. So they dug them out and laid them under about one hundred miles of railway. No one realized they were tearing apart a city more than four thousand years old.

In nineteen twenty-two, the archaeologist R D [Banerji](/bˈænəɹʤi/) was digging at a Buddhist stupa on another mound, far to the south, near the Indus River. Beneath it, he found something much older. The site was [Mohenjo](/mOhˈɛnʤO/) [daro](/dˈɑɹO/). In nineteen twenty-four, the world learned that both cities belonged to a lost civilization as old as ancient Egypt. About four thousand five hundred years ago, [Mohenjo](/mOhˈɛnʤO/) [daro](/dˈɑɹO/) already had straight streets laid out in a grid, drains, and bathrooms in most houses. Its name is usually translated as the Mound of the Dead.

=== 05_petra ===
Number six. Petra, Jordan.

In eighteen twelve, a young Swiss explorer named [Johann](/jˈOhɑn/) Ludwig [Burckhardt](/bˈɜɹkhɑɹt/) was travelling through what is now Jordan, dressed as a local and calling himself Sheikh [Ibrahim](/ˈɪbɹəhˌim/). He heard rumors of a ruined city hidden in the mountains. To get there without raising suspicion, he said he had made a vow to sacrifice a goat at the tomb of the prophet Aaron, at the far end of the valley.

On August twenty-second, eighteen twelve, his guide led him through a narrow canyon, and the cliffs opened onto temples and tombs carved straight into the rose-colored rock. [Burckhardt](/bˈɜɹkhɑɹt/) didn't dare take notes openly. His guides were already suspicious, and he feared they would take him for a magician hunting for treasure. So he looked, made his sacrifice, and left after sunset. It was enough. The lost capital of the Nabataeans was back on the map.

=== 06_pompeii ===
Number five. Pompeii, Italy.

In the year seventy-nine, Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the Roman city of Pompeii under meters of ash and pumice. Over the centuries, fields covered the ground above it, and the city was forgotten.

Here's the strange part. In the fifteen-nineties, the architect [Domenico](/dəmˈɛnikO/) [Fontana](/fɑntˈɑnə/) dug a canal to carry water from the [Sarno](/sˈɑɹnO/) River, and it ran straight through the buried city. His workers hit walls, wall paintings and inscriptions. And nobody followed up. For almost one hundred and fifty more years, Pompeii stayed hidden in plain sight. Proper excavations only began in seventeen forty-eight. Today you can walk its streets again, past bakeries, bathhouses, cart ruts in the stone, and election slogans still painted on the walls. It is one of the most detailed snapshots of everyday Roman life ever found, and it was almost found a century and a half earlier.

=== 07_machu_picchu ===
Number four. [Machu](/mˈɑʧu/) [Picchu](/pˈikʧu/), Peru.

On July twenty-fourth, nineteen eleven, an American explorer named [Hiram](/hˈIɹəm/) [Bingham](/bˈɪŋəm/) climbed a steep mountainside above the [Urubamba](/ˌuɹubˈɑmbə/) River in Peru. A local farmer, [Melchor](/mˈɛlkɔɹ/) [Arteaga](/ˌɑɹtAˈɑɡə/), had told him there were ruins up there. Near the top, a boy from a farming family led him the rest of the way. [Bingham](/bˈɪŋəm/) found a city of granite, spread across terraces in the clouds. He told the world he had found the lost city of the Incas.

But the city wasn't exactly lost. Families were already living there, growing crops on the ancient terraces. And on one of the walls was a name and a date: A [Lizárraga](/lisˈɑɹəɡə/), nineteen oh-two. A local farmer had been there nine years before [Bingham](/bˈɪŋəm/). [Machu](/mˈɑʧu/) [Picchu](/pˈikʧu/) was most likely built as a royal estate for the Inca emperor [Pachacuti](/pˌɑʧəkˈuti/) in the fourteen hundreds. The Spanish conquerors never found it. The outside world only did in nineteen eleven.

=== 08_ubar ===
Number three. [Ubar](/ˈubɑɹ/), Oman.

For thousands of years, frankincense was worth a fortune, and Arabian legends told of a rich city on the incense routes that vanished into the desert. Some called it [Ubar](/ˈubɑɹ/), others [Iram](/ˈiɹɑm/) of the Pillars. The explorer T E Lawrence called it the Atlantis of the Sands.

In nineteen eighty-four, the Space Shuttle Challenger scanned southern Oman with a radar that can see through dry sand. Later, a team led by filmmaker Nicholas [Clapp](/klˈæp/) studied those images, along with satellite photos, and spotted faint ancient caravan tracks, some running under huge dunes. Several of them led to a remote well called [Shisr](/ʃˈɪsəɹ/). In nineteen ninety-two, the team announced what they had dug up there: a fortress with towers and walls, built over a limestone cave whose roof seems to have collapsed. Scientists still debate whether this is the [Ubar](/ˈubɑɹ/) of legend. But it was found from space.

=== 09_valeriana ===
Number two. [Valeriana](/vəlˌɛɹiˈɑnə/), Mexico.

This one was found in twenty twenty-four. And it wasn't found in the jungle. It was found on a computer.

Luke Auld-Thomas, a P H D student at [Tulane](/tulˈAn/) University, was looking through old lidar data: laser scans, taken from an aircraft, that can map the ground beneath the trees. The scans had been made back in twenty thirteen, for an environmental project measuring the forests of southern Mexico. When he processed them, a hidden Maya city appeared in the state of [Campeche](/kæmpˈɛʧA/): temple pyramids, plazas, a [ballcourt](/bˈɔlkˌɔɹt/), a reservoir and thousands of buildings. Across the whole area, the team counted more than six thousand six hundred Maya structures. Auld-Thomas had driven past the place himself, never knowing what stood in the forest nearby. The city was named [Valeriana](/vəlˌɛɹiˈɑnə/), after a lagoon nearby. And it may be only the beginning. There could be many more cities still hiding under the trees.

=== 10_troy ===
Number one. Troy, Turkey.

For more than two thousand years, Troy was a story: the city of Helen, Achilles and the Trojan Horse. By the eighteen hundreds, many scholars doubted it had ever existed.

But on a hill called [Hisarlik](/hˌɪsɑɹlˈɪk/), in northwest Turkey, an Englishman named Frank [Calvert](/kˈælvəɹt/), who owned half of the mound, was convinced that Troy lay underneath. He just couldn't afford to dig. In eighteen seventy, the wealthy German businessman Heinrich [Schliemann](/ʃlˈimɑn/) could. With up to one hundred and sixty workers a day, he cut a huge trench through the hill, and in eighteen seventy-three he found a hoard of gold and silver that he called Priam's Treasure, after the king of Troy.

The problem: the hill held not one city, but nine, stacked on top of each other. The treasure turned out to be about one thousand years older than the Trojan War, and his trench destroyed much of what it cut through. Still, the legend had a real address. Most of that treasure is now in the [Pushkin](/pˈʊʃkɪn/) Museum in Moscow.

=== 11_atlantis ===
But there is one lost city that has never been found.

Around three hundred and sixty B C, the Greek philosopher Plato wrote about an island empire called Atlantis. In his dialogues [Timaeus](/tIˈmiəs/) and [Critias](/kɹˈɪtiəs/), he claimed the story reached the Athenian statesman Solon from an Egyptian priest. Atlantis, he wrote, lay beyond the Pillars of Heracles, what we now call the Strait of Gibraltar. It was larger than Libya and Asia combined. It grew powerful, it grew proud, and it went to war against Athens. Then, in a single day and night of misfortune, it sank into the sea, nine thousand years before Solon's time.

People have been hunting for it ever since. A map by [Athanasius](/ˌæθənˈAʃəs/) [Kircher](/kˈiɹkəɹ/), from sixteen sixty-nine, placed it in the middle of the Atlantic, drawn with south at the top. In eighteen eighty-two, a former U S congressman, [Ignatius](/ɪɡnˈAʃəs/) [Donnelly](/dˈɑnəli/), published a book arguing that Atlantis was real, and set off more than a century of searching.

The favorite real-world candidate is Santorini, in Greece. Around sixteen hundred B C, its volcano exploded in one of the most powerful eruptions in human history, burying the Bronze Age town of [Akrotiri](/ˌækɹOtˈɪɹi/) under ash. Its tsunamis likely struck the [Minoan](/mɪnˈOən/) civilization on nearby Crete.

But most historians think Plato invented Atlantis, as a warning about pride and power. Maybe Atlantis was never a place at all. But then again, people once said the same thing about Troy.
