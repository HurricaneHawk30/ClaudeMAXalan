=== 00_hook ===
For centuries, these cities were gone. Buried under ash. Swallowed by jungle. Hidden beneath the sand. Some were written off as legends. Then someone found them, often by accident. A storm. A search for stray cattle. A man knocking down a wall in his own house. These are ten lost cities that were found again. And at the end, the one lost city nobody has ever found.

=== 01_skara_brae ===
Number ten. Skara Brae, Scotland.

In 1850, a violent storm battered the Orkney Islands, off the north coast of Scotland. When it passed, the wind had stripped the sand from a large dune by the sea. Underneath were stone walls. Whole houses, linked by low passages, still standing after thousands of years.

Skara Brae is nearly 5,000 years old. That makes it older than the Egyptian pyramids, and older than Stonehenge. Inside the houses, the furniture is still there, because it was made of stone: beds, a hearth in the middle of the floor, and watertight boxes set into the ground, possibly for keeping fishing bait. Around 2,500 BC, the people of Skara Brae slowly abandoned their village. Nobody knows exactly why. The sand covered it, and kept it safe, until the storm brought it back.

=== 02_cliff_palace ===
Number nine. Cliff Palace, Colorado, USA.

On December 18th, 1888, two cowboys, Richard Wetherill and his brother-in-law Charlie Mason, were riding across Mesa Verde in southwestern Colorado, looking for lost cattle. From the rim of a canyon, they spotted something that should not have been there: an entire town tucked under the overhang of a cliff. Towers, walls and windows, silent and empty.

They had found Cliff Palace. Ancestral Puebloan people built it around 800 years ago, fitting it into a natural alcove in the sandstone. It has about 150 rooms and 23 kivas, round sunken rooms used for ceremonies, and around 100 people lived here. But by about 1300, its builders had moved away to the south, and the town stood empty for nearly 600 years, until two men looking for their cows rode by.

=== 03_derinkuyu ===
Number eight. Derinkuyu, Turkey.

In 1963, a man in the town of Derinkuyu, in central Turkey, was renovating his house. He knocked down a wall in his basement and found a room behind it. Behind that room was another one. And then another. He had stumbled into the entrance of an entire city, carved into the soft volcanic rock beneath his feet.

Derinkuyu goes down about 85 meters and has at least 18 levels. It could shelter up to 20,000 people, along with their animals and supplies. There are stables, cellars, wells, schools and chapels, all connected by tunnels. More than 50 air shafts kept the air fresh, and huge stone doors could be rolled across the passages to seal them from the inside. Parts of it may date back to the 8th century BC, and people were still using some of its rooms in the 19th century.

=== 04_mohenjo_daro ===
Number seven. Mohenjo-daro, Pakistan.

In the 19th century, British engineers building the railway between Lahore and Multan needed ballast for the track. Near the village of Harappa, they found a huge mound full of old, perfectly baked bricks. So they dug them out and laid them under about 100 miles of railway. No one realized they were tearing apart a city more than 4,000 years old.

In 1922, the archaeologist R. D. Banerji was digging at a Buddhist stupa on another mound, far to the south, near the Indus River. Beneath it, he found something much older. The site was Mohenjo-daro. In 1924, the world learned that both cities belonged to a lost civilization as old as ancient Egypt. About 4,500 years ago, Mohenjo-daro already had straight streets laid out in a grid, drains, and bathrooms in most houses. Its name is usually translated as the Mound of the Dead.

=== 05_petra ===
Number six. Petra, Jordan.

In 1812, a young Swiss explorer named Johann Ludwig Burckhardt was travelling through what is now Jordan, dressed as a local and calling himself Sheikh Ibrahim. He heard rumors of a ruined city hidden in the mountains. To get there without raising suspicion, he said he had made a vow to sacrifice a goat at the tomb of the prophet Aaron, at the far end of the valley.

On August 22nd, 1812, his guide led him through a narrow canyon, and the cliffs opened onto temples and tombs carved straight into the rose-colored rock. Burckhardt didn't dare take notes openly. His guides were already suspicious, and he feared they would take him for a magician hunting for treasure. So he looked, made his sacrifice, and left after sunset. It was enough. The lost capital of the Nabataeans was back on the map.

=== 06_pompeii ===
Number five. Pompeii, Italy.

In the year 79, Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the Roman city of Pompeii under meters of ash and pumice. Over the centuries, fields covered the ground above it, and the city was forgotten.

Here's the strange part. In the 1590s, the architect Domenico Fontana dug a canal to carry water from the Sarno River, and it ran straight through the buried city. His workers hit walls, wall paintings and inscriptions. And nobody followed up. For almost 150 more years, Pompeii stayed hidden in plain sight. Proper excavations only began in 1748. Today you can walk its streets again, past bakeries, bathhouses, cart ruts in the stone, and election slogans still painted on the walls. It is one of the most detailed snapshots of everyday Roman life ever found, and it was almost found a century and a half earlier.

=== 07_machu_picchu ===
Number four. Machu Picchu, Peru.

On July 24th, 1911, an American explorer named Hiram Bingham climbed a steep mountainside above the Urubamba River in Peru. A local farmer, Melchor Arteaga, had told him there were ruins up there. Near the top, a boy from a farming family led him the rest of the way. Bingham found a city of granite, spread across terraces in the clouds. He told the world he had found the lost city of the Incas.

But the city wasn't exactly lost. Families were already living there, growing crops on the ancient terraces. And on one of the walls was a name and a date: A. Lizárraga, 1902. A local farmer had been there nine years before Bingham. Machu Picchu was most likely built as a royal estate for the Inca emperor Pachacuti in the 1400s. The Spanish conquerors never found it. The outside world only did in 1911.

=== 08_ubar ===
Number three. Ubar, Oman.

For thousands of years, frankincense was worth a fortune, and Arabian legends told of a rich city on the incense routes that vanished into the desert. Some called it Ubar, others Iram of the Pillars. The explorer T. E. Lawrence called it the Atlantis of the Sands.

In 1984, the Space Shuttle Challenger scanned southern Oman with a radar that can see through dry sand. Later, a team led by filmmaker Nicholas Clapp studied those images, along with satellite photos, and spotted faint ancient caravan tracks, some running under huge dunes. Several of them led to a remote well called Shisr. In 1992, the team announced what they had dug up there: a fortress with towers and walls, built over a limestone cave whose roof seems to have collapsed. Scientists still debate whether this is the Ubar of legend. But it was found from space.

=== 09_valeriana ===
Number two. Valeriana, Mexico.

This one was found in 2024. And it wasn't found in the jungle. It was found on a computer.

Luke Auld-Thomas, a PhD student at Tulane University, was looking through old lidar data: laser scans, taken from an aircraft, that can map the ground beneath the trees. The scans had been made back in 2013, for an environmental project measuring the forests of southern Mexico. When he processed them, a hidden Maya city appeared in the state of Campeche: temple pyramids, plazas, a ballcourt, a reservoir and thousands of buildings. Across the whole area, the team counted more than 6,600 Maya structures. Auld-Thomas had driven past the place himself, never knowing what stood in the forest nearby. The city was named Valeriana, after a lagoon nearby. And it may be only the beginning. There could be many more cities still hiding under the trees.

=== 10_troy ===
Number one. Troy, Turkey.

For more than 2,000 years, Troy was a story: the city of Helen, Achilles and the Trojan Horse. By the 1800s, many scholars doubted it had ever existed.

But on a hill called Hisarlik, in northwest Turkey, an Englishman named Frank Calvert, who owned half of the mound, was convinced that Troy lay underneath. He just couldn't afford to dig. In 1870, the wealthy German businessman Heinrich Schliemann could. With up to 160 workers a day, he cut a huge trench through the hill, and in 1873 he found a hoard of gold and silver that he called Priam's Treasure, after the king of Troy.

The problem: the hill held not one city, but nine, stacked on top of each other. The treasure turned out to be about 1,000 years older than the Trojan War, and his trench destroyed much of what it cut through. Still, the legend had a real address. Most of that treasure is now in the Pushkin Museum in Moscow.

=== 11_atlantis ===
But there is one lost city that has never been found.

Around 360 BC, the Greek philosopher Plato wrote about an island empire called Atlantis. In his dialogues Timaeus and Critias, he claimed the story reached the Athenian statesman Solon from an Egyptian priest. Atlantis, he wrote, lay beyond the Pillars of Heracles, what we now call the Strait of Gibraltar. It was larger than Libya and Asia combined. It grew powerful, it grew proud, and it went to war against Athens. Then, in a single day and night of misfortune, it sank into the sea, 9,000 years before Solon's time.

People have been hunting for it ever since. A map by Athanasius Kircher, from 1669, placed it in the middle of the Atlantic, drawn with south at the top. In 1882, a former U.S. congressman, Ignatius Donnelly, published a book arguing that Atlantis was real, and set off more than a century of searching.

The favorite real-world candidate is Santorini, in Greece. Around 1600 BC, its volcano exploded in one of the most powerful eruptions in human history, burying the Bronze Age town of Akrotiri under ash. Its tsunamis likely struck the Minoan civilization on nearby Crete.

But most historians think Plato invented Atlantis, as a warning about pride and power. Maybe Atlantis was never a place at all. But then again, people once said the same thing about Troy.
