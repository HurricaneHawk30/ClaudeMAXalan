=== 00_hook ===
There are places on Earth where white stone pours down a hillside like frozen water. Where a river runs red. Where the pools are yellow and green, and more acidic than battery acid. Scientists even use some of them to practice for Mars. These are ten places on our own planet that look like they belong on another one. And at number one: a place so hostile that scientists are still arguing whether anything can live there.

=== 01_pamukkale ===
Number ten. Pamukkale, Turkey.

From a distance, it looks like a hillside covered in snow, even in the heat of summer. Up close, it's stone. Hot springs at the top of a cliff almost 200 meters high carry water full of dissolved calcium carbonate. When the water reaches the air, carbon dioxide escapes, and the mineral is left behind as a soft white gel that slowly hardens into rock, called travertine. Over thousands of years, it has built a staircase of white terraces and pools, some less than a meter tall, others as high as six. The Turks call it Pamukkale: Cotton Castle.

People have bathed here for more than two thousand years. The ancient Greek city of Hierapolis was built right on top of it. Today, you walk the terraces barefoot, and the hotels that once stood on them were torn down to save them.

=== 02_danxia ===
Number nine. The Rainbow Mountains of Zhangye, China.

In Gansu Province, in northwest China, the hills are striped. Red, orange, yellow, white, even greenish, layer after layer, as if someone painted them. They're real. Over millions of years, sand and minerals settled here in layers, each with its own mix of iron and other elements. The layers were pressed into sandstone, then pushed up and tilted by the movement of tectonic plates. Wind and rain did the rest, carving them into ridges that look like frozen waves of color.

The colors glow brightest just after rain, and at sunset, when low light makes the stripes burn. This kind of landscape is called Danxia, and Zhangye's is one of the most famous in China. Today, it's a national geopark crossed by wooden walkways, so that visitors don't wear the colors away.

=== 03_spotted ===
Number eight. Spotted Lake, Canada.

Near the town of Osoyoos, in British Columbia, beside a highway, there's a lake that looks like the skin of a leopard. It's packed with minerals, mostly magnesium sulfate, along with calcium and sodium sulfates. Every summer, most of the water evaporates, and what's left breaks into dozens of separate pools, each a different color, depending on the minerals inside it. Between them, the hardened minerals form natural walkways.

For the Syilx people of the Okanagan, this is Kliluk, a sacred medicine lake that has been used for healing for centuries. During the First World War, its minerals were dug out to help make ammunition. In 2001, the Okanagan Nation bought the lake and the land around it. Today, it's fenced off. You can look at it from the road, but the lake itself is off-limits.

=== 04_deadvlei ===
Number seven. Deadvlei, Namibia.

In the Namib Desert, often called the oldest desert in the world, there's a flat white clay pan surrounded by some of the highest sand dunes on Earth. The tallest one nearby, nicknamed Big Daddy, rises about 325 meters. And standing on the white floor are dozens of black, twisted trees. They're camel thorn trees, and they've been dead for centuries.

Long ago, the Tsauchab River flooded this pan, and trees grew around its shallow pools. Then the climate turned drier, the dunes moved in, and the pan was cut off from the river. The trees died, most likely six to nine hundred years ago. But they never fell, and they never rotted. The air is so dry that nothing breaks them down. The sun just burned them black. Orange dunes, white ground, black trees, deep blue sky. Almost every photo from here looks fake.

=== 05_socotra ===
Number six. Socotra, Yemen.

In the Arabian Sea, between the Horn of Africa and Arabia, lies an island that has been cut off from the mainland for millions of years. Life here went its own way. Of the 825 plant species on Socotra, 37 percent grow nowhere else on Earth. For reptiles, it's 90 percent. For land snails, 95.

The most famous resident is the dragon's blood tree. It grows in the shape of an open umbrella, with thick branches turned upward and a dense crown on top, a shape that is thought to help it catch moisture from the mist and shade its own roots. Cut the bark, and it bleeds a dark red resin, which has been traded for centuries as a dye and a medicine. Nearby grow bottle trees with swollen trunks, like something from a children's drawing. Socotra became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2008, and it's still one of the hardest places on Earth to visit.

=== 06_uyuni ===
Number five. Salar de Uyuni, Bolivia.

High in the Andes, at about 3,650 meters, lies the largest salt flat in the world: more than 10,000 square kilometers of white salt, left behind by ancient lakes that dried up long ago. It's so flat that across the whole area, the height changes by less than a meter.

In the dry season, the salt cracks into hexagons that stretch all the way to the horizon. But after the rains, a layer of water only a few centimeters deep covers the flat. When the wind stops, it turns into the largest mirror on Earth. Clouds float under your feet, and the horizon disappears.

That flatness is useful beyond photos. Because the surface is so big, so bright and so even, space agencies use it to calibrate the instruments of Earth-observing satellites. And under the salt lies one of the largest lithium deposits on the planet, the metal inside the batteries of phones and electric cars.

=== 07_richat ===
Number four. The Eye of the Sahara, Mauritania.

In the desert of northwestern Mauritania, there's a giant target drawn on the ground: rings within rings, about 40 kilometers across. It's so big that from the ground, you can barely tell it's there. But from orbit, it's impossible to miss. Space shuttle crews used it as a landmark.

It's called the Richat Structure. For a long time, scientists thought it might be a crater, left by an asteroid. But there's no sign of an impact. Today, geologists think it was a dome: layers of rock pushed up from below, like a blister. Over millions of years, wind and sand wore the top away and exposed the layers in rings, like the inside of a cut onion.

Because it looks so perfect, it attracts wild theories, even claims that it's the lost city of Atlantis. There's no evidence for that. The real story, a mountain erased by wind until only its rings remained, is strange enough.

=== 08_riotinto ===
Number three. Rio Tinto, Spain.

In Andalusia, in southern Spain, a river runs red. Not muddy brown, but deep red and orange, like rust. Its water is about as acidic as vinegar and full of dissolved iron and heavy metals. People have mined copper, silver and gold along it for about five thousand years, and one of the biggest mining companies in the world, Rio Tinto, took its name from this river. But research suggests the river was acidic long before anyone started digging.

For a long time, people assumed nothing could live in water like this. They were wrong. The river is full of microbes that live on iron and sulfur, and they help give it its color.

That's why NASA is interested. Minerals found around the Rio Tinto, like one called jarosite, have also been found on Mars. So scientists have come here to test drills and life-detection instruments, practicing the search for life on Mars, in a river in Spain.

=== 09_devon ===
Number two. Devon Island, Canada.

In the Canadian Arctic lies the largest uninhabited island on Earth. No towns, no roads, almost no plants. Just cold, dry, brown rock under a grey sky. Millions of years ago, a meteorite slammed into it and left a crater about 20 kilometers wide: Haughton Crater.

Cold, dry, cratered and empty: that's a pretty good description of Mars. So since 1997, scientists working with NASA have spent many summers here, on the Haughton-Mars Project. They test rovers, spacesuits and equipment, and practice how a crew could live and work on another planet. Nearby, the Mars Society built a research station where small crews live like Mars explorers for weeks at a time.

If humans ever walk on Mars, part of what they know will have been learned here, on an island where nobody lives.

=== 10_dallol ===
Number one. Dallol, Ethiopia.

In the Danakil Depression, in northeastern Ethiopia, the ground lies about 125 meters below sea level, and it's one of the hottest places on Earth. Deep below, magma heats water that rises through thick layers of salt. Where it breaks the surface, it builds a landscape that doesn't look like Earth at all: bright yellow crusts, green and orange pools, white salt chimneys, all hissing and bubbling.

The colors come from chemistry. The water can be close to boiling, and extremely acidic. NASA describes pools here with a pH of about 0.25, more acidic than battery acid. Scientists come to Dallol to answer one question: is there a limit to where life can survive? Some studies found tiny microbes in the pools. Others found nothing at all in the most extreme ones. The debate isn't over.

And yet people work here. On the salt flats nearby, Afar miners still cut blocks of salt by hand and load them onto camel caravans, as they have for centuries.
