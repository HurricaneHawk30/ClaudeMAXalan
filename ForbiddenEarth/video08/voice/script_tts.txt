=== 00_hook ===
The world's famous wonders are crowded. Long lines, selfie sticks, tour buses. But some of the most beautiful places on Earth are almost empty. They're too far away, too hard to reach, or protected by rules so strict that only a few people get in. A pink lake. A river of five colors. A bay that glows in the dark. And at number one: a cave so big it has its own jungle inside, found by one man who then lost it for almost twenty years.

=== 01_crooked ===
Number ten. The Crooked Forest, Poland.

Near the small town of [Gryfino](/ɡɹɪfˈinO/), in western Poland, there's a pine forest where something went wrong. Around four hundred trees grow out of the ground, then bend sharply sideways, almost flat, before curving back up toward the sky. Every one of them bends the same way, to the north. All around them, the other pines grow perfectly straight.

The trees were planted around nineteen thirty, and nobody knows for sure what happened to them. Some people blame heavy snow. Others think tanks rolled over them during the Second World War. The most popular theory is that foresters bent the young trees on purpose, to grow curved wood for furniture or boats, and then the war came, and nobody came back to finish the job.

No one has proven any of it. The forest just stands there, keeping its secret.

=== 02_fingal ===
Number nine. [Fingal](/fˈɪŋɡəl/)'s Cave, Scotland.

Off the west coast of Scotland lies Staffa, a tiny island with no people on it. Its cliffs look like they were built, not formed: thousands of six-sided stone columns, packed together like a giant honeycomb. They're basalt, made when lava cooled slowly and cracked into these shapes millions of years ago.

On one side of the island, the sea has hollowed out a cave between the columns. It's tall like a cathedral, and when waves roll in, the whole cave booms and echoes. In seventeen seventy-two, the naturalist Joseph Banks visited and made it famous. And in eighteen twenty-nine, a young composer named [Felix](/fˈilɪks/) [Mendelssohn](/mˈɛndəlsən/) came here by boat. The sound of the sea in the cave stayed with him, and he turned it into music: the Hebrides Overture.

Today, small boats bring visitors when the sea is calm. But the weather here is wild, and many trips never make it.

=== 03_hillier ===
Number eight. Lake Hillier, Australia.

Off the south coast of Western Australia, on Middle Island, there's a lake that is bright pink. Not a pale, pinkish tint. From above, it looks like strawberry milk, separated from the deep blue ocean by only a thin strip of sand and forest.

The explorer Matthew Flinders saw it in January eighteen oh two, after climbing the highest hill on the island. The color is believed to come from tiny salt-loving algae, [Dunaliella](/dˌunəliˈɛlə/) salina, which produce a red pigment to protect themselves from the sun.

The lake is only about six hundred meters long, and the island has no roads, no hotels and no regular ferry. For almost everyone who has seen it, the view comes from a scenic flight. From the shore, the water looks a softer pink. From the sky, it looks unreal.

=== 04_kelimutu ===
Number seven. [Kelimutu](/kˌɛlimˈutu/), Indonesia.

On the island of [Flores](/flˈɔɹɛs/), at the top of a volcano, there are three crater lakes side by side. And they don't stay the same color. Over the years, they have turned turquoise, green, red, brown, and even black. Sometimes one lake changes while the others stay the same.

Scientists explain it with chemistry. Gas and minerals rising from the volcano below change the water, and with it, the color. But for the local [Lio](/lˈiO/) people, the explanation is older. They believe these lakes are where souls go after death. One lake for the young, one for the old, and one for those who did evil.

To see them, you climb to the crater rim before dawn and wait for the mist to lift. Then, one by one, the three lakes appear.

=== 05_marble ===
Number six. The Marble Caves, Chile.

Deep in Patagonia, on a huge glacial lake called [General](/hˌAnəɹˈɑl/) [Carrera](/kəɹˈɛɹə/), there are caves made of marble. Over more than six thousand years, the waves of the lake have worn tunnels, arches and pillars into the stone. The walls are streaked with white, grey and blue, and they reflect the color of the water, which changes with the seasons.

The only way to get inside is by small boat, on calm days. Inside, the light bouncing off the turquoise water makes the marble look like it's moving.

Getting here is a journey of its own. It's a long drive on remote roads through Patagonia, and the lake is so big that it crosses into Argentina, where it has a different name. Most travelers in South America never come this far south.

=== 06_jellyfish ===
Number five. Jellyfish Lake, Palau.

In the Pacific Ocean, on a jungle-covered island in Palau, there's a lake full of jellyfish. Millions of them. They're golden jellyfish, a type found nowhere else, and their sting is so weak that people can swim among them.

The jellyfish carry tiny algae inside their bodies, which turn sunlight into food. So every day, the whole swarm follows the sun across the lake.

Then, in twenty sixteen, a severe drought caused by El [Niño](/nˈinjO/) changed the lake's water. The jellyfish vanished. By May of that year, the last one was gone. Scientists watched and waited. Slowly, as the lake cooled again, the jellyfish returned, and by twenty nineteen, there were millions once more. Today, swimmers can go back in, with snorkels only, because air bubbles from scuba tanks could hurt the jellyfish.

=== 07_hidden_beach ===
Number four. The Hidden Beach, Mexico.

Off the Pacific coast of Mexico lie the [Marieta](/mˌɑɹiˈɛtə/) Islands. In the early nineteen hundreds, they were empty, so the Mexican government used them for military target practice. Bombs and explosions tore at the rock.

Many people believe that's how one of the strangest beaches in the world was made: a round hole in the top of an island, with a perfect little beach at the bottom, open to the sky. Nobody can say for certain how it formed, but the only way in is to swim through a short tunnel from the open sea.

Too many tourists came, and the beach began to suffer. In twenty sixteen, it was closed to let it recover. When it reopened, visitors were limited to only about a hundred a day. You need a permit, a guide, and a good pair of lungs.

=== 08_mosquito_bay ===
Number three. Mosquito Bay, Puerto Rico.

On the island of [Vieques](/viˈAkɛs/), there's a bay that glows. At night, every movement in the water lights up blue and green: a paddle, a fish, a falling raindrop. Guinness World Records lists it as the brightest bioluminescent bay in the world, with up to seven hundred thousand tiny glowing organisms in every gallon of water.

For decades, most visitors never came here at all. Much of [Vieques](/viˈAkɛs/) was used by the U S Navy as a bombing range, and the bombing only stopped in two thousand and three.

Today, the bay is a protected nature reserve. Swimming is banned, and so are motorboats. You can only go in on a guided tour by kayak or electric boat, on a dark night, ideally around the new moon. Then you dip your hand into the water, and it lights up like stars.

=== 09_cano_cristales ===
Number two. [Caño](/kˈɑnjO/) [Cristales](/kɹɪstˈɑlɛs/), Colombia.

In a remote mountain range in Colombia, there's a river that, for a few months a year, turns into a rainbow. Red, yellow, green, blue and black. Locals call it the river of five colors, or the liquid rainbow.

The red comes from a water plant that grows on the riverbed, and in the right season, with the right amount of water and sunlight, it turns a deep, vivid red. Yellow sand, green algae and black rock complete the picture.

For decades, almost nobody could see it. The region was caught up in Colombia's long armed conflict and closed to visitors. It only reopened gradually in the late two thousands. Today, it's still hard to reach, usually by small plane, and the number of visitors is controlled to protect the river.

=== 10_son_doong ===
Number one. [Hang](/hˈɑŋ/) Son [Doong](/dˈuŋ/), Vietnam.

Around nineteen ninety, deep in the jungle of central Vietnam, a local man named Ho [Khanh](/kˈɑn/) was out in the forest when he found the entrance to a cave. He could hear a river roaring inside, and cold mist blowing out. He didn't go in. And afterwards, he couldn't find it again.

For almost twenty years, the cave stayed lost. Then, in two thousand and eight, he found the entrance once more. In two thousand and nine, he led a British and Vietnamese caving team inside, and they realized what he had found: one of the largest cave passages on Earth, big enough to fit a skyscraper.

In places, the roof has collapsed, letting sunlight pour in. Under those openings, a jungle grows inside the cave, with trees, birds and clouds of mist. Today, only a small number of people can enter each year, on a guided expedition that takes several days. It's a whole hidden world, and for most of history, nobody knew it was there.
