 165.7 High Brazil Atlantic Ocean Long before Google, sailors' charts showed a round island off the west coast of Ireland, often with a river or a strait cutting it in two.
 178.5 Its name was Brazil, or High Brazil.
 181.9 It first appeared on a chart in 1325, and for more than 500 years, mapmakers kept drawing it.

 198.0 Explorers went looking for it.

 207.5 The island was never found, but mapmakers were slow to give it up.

 369.0 Burmeja, Gulf of Mexico In the 1500s, Spanish mapmakers marked a small island called Burmeja off the north coast of Mexico's Yucatan Peninsula.
 381.5 For centuries, it stayed on the maps.
 384.8 Nobody thought much about it until it turned out to be worth a fortune.

 410.8 So Mexico went looking.
 412.7 In 1997, a survey found nothing.
 415.3 In 2009, scientists from Mexico's National University searched again with ships, aircraft, and underwater sensors.
 424.0 No island, not even a sunken one.
 427.2 Rumors spread that it had been secretly blown up.
 430.2 Scientists think the answer is simpler.
 432.5 It was probably never there at all, a mistake on an old map that survived for almost 500 years.

 523.0 Duga Radar, Ukraine In 1976, radio listeners all over the world started hearing a strange sound on the shortwave bands, a fast, sharp tapping, like a bird pecking at a tree.
 537.9 They called it the Russian woodpecker.
 540.3 It drowned out radio stations and even disturbed air traffic channels, and no one would admit where it came from.
 548.2 It came from a forest in Soviet Ukraine, near the town of Chernobyl.
 553.5 There, the Soviets had built Duga, a giant over -the -horizon radar designed to spot American missiles thousands of kilometers away.
 562.9 Its receiving antenna was a wall of steel about 150 meters high and around 700 meters long.
 571.8 To hide something that huge, the Soviets used a simple trick.

 592.3 The giant steel wall still stands in the Chernobyl exclusion zone, with the forest growing back around it.
