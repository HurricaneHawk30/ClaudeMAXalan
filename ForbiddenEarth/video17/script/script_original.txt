=== 00_hook ===
Some places on Earth won't be here much longer. A sea that drops more than a meter every year. A capital city sinking so fast that its government is building a new one. A village in the Alps that vanished in a single afternoon. And at number one: a whole country where about half the people have entered a lottery for a chance to leave.

=== 01_glacier_np ===
Number ten. Glacier National Park, USA.

The park in Montana got its name for a reason. Around 1850, there were about a hundred and fifty glaciers here, hanging in the high valleys of the Rocky Mountains.

Scientists from the U.S. Geological Survey have measured them for decades, using old photographs, aerial surveys and satellites. By their latest count, only twenty-six were still big enough to be called glaciers. Since the 1960s, some of them have shrunk by more than eighty percent.

You can still hike to Grinnell Glacier and see blue ice above a turquoise lake. But compare a photo from a hundred years ago with one from today, and you'll see a mountain that's losing its ice. Scientists say the park's glaciers could disappear within our lifetime.

=== 02_kilimanjaro ===
Number nine. The glaciers of Kilimanjaro, Tanzania.

Kilimanjaro is the highest mountain in Africa, and one of the strangest sights on the planet: snow and ice sitting almost on the equator, above the hot savanna.

But that ice is going fast. When it was first mapped in 1912, it covered about twelve square kilometers. By 2007, around eighty-five percent of it was gone. What's left is broken into small, shrinking fields with steep walls of ice, melting and evaporating in the sun.

Scientists who study the mountain expect most of the remaining ice to disappear around 2040. One day soon, climbers will reach the top of Africa and find only bare rock.

=== 03_aral_sea ===
Number eight. The Aral Sea, Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan.

In the 1960s, the Aral Sea was one of the four biggest lakes in the world. Fishing towns lined its shores. Then the Soviet Union diverted the two rivers that fed it, to grow cotton in the desert.

The sea began to die. Over the next fifty years, it lost around ninety percent of its water. It broke apart into smaller, saltier lakes. The southern part turned into a new desert, the Aralkum, full of salt and chemicals from the fields. Rusty fishing boats still sit in the sand, kilometers from any water.

But there's a twist. In 2005, Kazakhstan built a dam to save the small northern part of the sea. The water rose again, and fish came back. The rest of the Aral Sea is still gone.

=== 04_dead_sea ===
Number seven. The Dead Sea, Israel, Jordan and the West Bank.

Its shore is the lowest point on dry land, more than four hundred meters below sea level. The water is so salty that you float on it without trying.

And it's shrinking. Most of the Jordan River's water is now used before it ever reaches the sea, and mineral factories evaporate even more. The level drops by more than a meter every year. Since the 1960s, the Dead Sea has lost about a third of its surface.

As the water retreats, it leaves behind something dangerous. Fresh water dissolves the salt underground, and the ground collapses. In 1990, there were about a hundred sinkholes along the shore. Today there are more than six thousand. Beaches, roads and even resorts had to be abandoned.

=== 05_venice ===
Number six. Venice, Italy.

Venice is built on wooden piles driven into a lagoon, and for centuries, it has been slowly sinking, while the sea around it slowly rises.

Floods are part of life here. In November 2019, the water reached one meter eighty-seven, the second-highest flood ever recorded. Water poured into St. Mark's Basilica, and the city declared a state of emergency. Since 2020, a system of seventy-eight giant gates, called MOSE, rises from the seabed to hold back the highest tides.

Venice faces another flood too: people. Since 2024, day-trippers have to pay an entry fee on the busiest days. In 2026, the fee covered sixty days. Fewer people live in the old city than ever, and on some days, visitors outnumber residents.

=== 06_great_barrier_reef ===
Number five. The Great Barrier Reef, Australia.

It's the largest living structure on Earth, more than two thousand kilometers of coral off the coast of Queensland. You can see it from space.

But coral is fragile. When the water gets too hot, corals push out the tiny algae that live inside them, turn white, and can starve. This is called bleaching. The reef has had mass bleaching in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, 2024 and 2025.

In 2025, scientists reported the biggest one-year loss of coral in almost forty years of monitoring, in two of the reef's three regions. The latest survey found the first signs of recovery. Reefs can bounce back, but only if they get enough calm years between the heatwaves, and those calm years are getting shorter.

=== 07_isle_de_jean_charles ===
Number four. Isle de Jean Charles, USA.

In the 1950s, this island in the Louisiana bayou was around eighteen kilometers long and eight wide. It was home to Native American families who fished, trapped and farmed there for generations.

Since 1955, it has lost about ninety-eight percent of its land. Canals for oil and gas, sinking ground, salt water and stronger storms have eaten it away. Today it's a thin strip, connected to the mainland by a single road that floods.

In 2016, the U.S. government gave forty-eight million dollars to move the community to safer ground, the first time federal money was used to relocate an American community because of climate change. Many families have already moved to a new site, on former farmland about an hour's drive north. Their island is slowly slipping into the Gulf.

=== 08_jakarta ===
Number three. Jakarta, Indonesia.

Jakarta is one of the biggest cities on Earth, and one of the fastest sinking. Millions of people pump water from under the ground, and as the water comes out, the ground sinks.

Researchers in Indonesia say the city is sinking by around three and a half centimeters a year on average, and much faster in some northern districts. Over the past forty years, parts of North Jakarta have dropped by up to four meters. Around forty percent of that area now lies below sea level, protected by walls that keep getting higher.

The problem is so serious that Indonesia decided to build a new capital, Nusantara, on the island of Borneo. Jakarta isn't going anywhere. But some of its neighborhoods already flood at every high tide.

=== 09_blatten ===
Number two. Blatten, Switzerland.

Blatten was a small, quiet village in a valley in the Swiss Alps, with old wooden houses, a church and about three hundred people. In May 2025, the mountain above it began to crack, dropping rocks onto the Birch Glacier below.

Scientists watched the glacier speed up. On May 19, the whole village was evacuated. Even the cows and sheep were flown out by helicopter.

Nine days later, on May 28, almost the entire glacier collapsed. Millions of tons of ice, rock and mud thundered into the valley and buried most of Blatten within minutes. Because people had left in time, almost everyone was safe. As mountains warm and permafrost melts, scientists warn that other Alpine villages may face the same danger.

=== 10_tuvalu ===
Number one. Tuvalu.

Tuvalu is one of the smallest countries on Earth: nine islands of coral and sand in the Pacific Ocean, with around eleven thousand people. Most of the land is less than two or three meters above the sea.

As the ocean rises, storms reach further inland, salt water spoils the gardens, and high tides flood homes. Scientists warn that much of the capital, Funafuti, could be under water at high tide within decades.

So, in 2023, Tuvalu signed a treaty with Australia. Every year, two hundred and eighty Tuvaluans can win a visa to live there for good. When the first lottery opened in 2025, more than five thousand people entered, about half of the country. Tuvalu is building new land to protect its capital. It is also preparing for something no country has ever had to plan: how to stay a nation, even if its land disappears.
