=== 00_hook ===
Somebody built these places. We just don't fully know how, or why. Giant caves carved by hand that no history book ever mentions. A stone block as heavy as four fully loaded jumbo jets. A stone that was moved across almost the whole of Britain. And at number one: a temple older than farming, older than writing, older than almost anything humans ever built.

=== 01_longyou ===
Number ten. The Longyou Caves, China.

In 1992, in a village in eastern China, a farmer named Wu Anai wanted to know how deep the local ponds really were. People had always said they had no bottom. So he and his neighbors bought a water pump and drained one.

Under the water, there was no bottom of mud. There was a huge hall carved into solid sandstone, with straight walls, sloping ceilings and stone pillars. Then they drained more ponds, and found more. Twenty-four caves in all, some of them more than thirty meters across.

Every wall and ceiling is covered in neat, parallel chisel marks, as if the whole thing was carved by one careful hand. Objects found inside suggest the caves are more than two thousand years old. But China kept detailed records for thousands of years, and not one of them mentions these caves. Who dug them, and why, is still unknown.

=== 02_carnac ===
Number nine. The Carnac Stones, France.

On the coast of Brittany, in the northwest of France, more than three thousand standing stones stretch across the fields in long, straight rows, some of them running for over a kilometer.

They were raised by Neolithic people, most of them between about 4800 and 3500 BC, long before Stonehenge. Some stones are taller than a person. Each one had to be cut from granite, moved, and stood upright without metal tools or the wheel.

Local legend says they're a Roman army, turned to stone by a saint. Archaeologists have other ideas: a calendar, a place of ritual, markers of land or of the dead. Nobody really knows. In July 2025, the megaliths of Carnac and the Morbihan coast became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The question of why they were built is still open.

=== 03_sacsayhuaman ===
Number eight. Sacsayhuamán, Peru.

On a hill above Cusco, the old capital of the Inca Empire, stand three giant zigzag walls made of enormous stones. Some of them weigh more than a hundred tons. The largest is more than eight meters tall.

What amazes people isn't just their size. It's the fit. Each stone has a different shape, with many angles, and each one is cut to lock perfectly into the stones around it, with no mortar at all. In many places, you couldn't push a sheet of paper into the joints.

The Inca built it in the fifteenth century, without iron tools, without wheels, and without horses. Researchers think they used stone hammers, ramps, ropes and huge teams of workers. But exactly how they shaped and fitted blocks that heavy so precisely is still debated today.

=== 04_plain_of_jars ===
Number seven. The Plain of Jars, Laos.

In the hills of northern Laos, thousands of giant stone jars are scattered across the landscape. Some stand alone. Others are in groups of hundreds. The largest are about three meters tall and can weigh up to twenty tons.

For a long time, no one could say how old they were. In 2021, researchers dated the soil beneath some of them and found the jars may have been put in place as early as 1240 to 660 BC. Human remains found nearby suggest they had something to do with the dead. But how these stone giants were carved, moved, and what exactly they held is still a mystery.

There's another danger here. During the Vietnam War, this region was heavily bombed, and unexploded bombs still lie in the ground. Visitors can only walk on paths that have been cleared.

=== 05_yonaguni ===
Number six. The Yonaguni Monument, Japan.

In 1986, a diver looking for hammerhead sharks off Yonaguni, one of the westernmost islands of Japan, saw something strange on the seabed: huge flat terraces, sharp right angles, and steps, like an underwater pyramid.

One Japanese marine geologist spent years studying it and became convinced it was built by people, thousands of years ago, when the sea level was lower and this was dry land. If he's right, it would rewrite the history of the region.

But most geologists disagree. They say the rock here naturally splits along straight lines, and that waves and earthquakes could have shaped those steps. No convincing tools or pottery have ever been found there. Is it a lost monument, or one of nature's best illusions? Divers still argue about it.

=== 06_nan_madol ===
Number five. Nan Madol, Micronesia.

Off the coast of Pohnpei, a small island in the western Pacific, about a hundred artificial islands sit in a shallow lagoon, connected by canals. Their walls are made of long black columns of basalt, stacked like logs in a cabin.

This was the center of power of the Saudeleur dynasty, who ruled the island until around 1628. Most of the stone structures were built between about 1200 and 1500 AD. Altogether, the stone is estimated to weigh around seven hundred and fifty thousand tons, and some single columns weigh as much as fifty tons.

The basalt came from quarries on the other side of the island. How a few tens of thousands of people moved all that stone across water, without cranes or metal tools, no one knows. Local legend says two sorcerers made the stones fly.

=== 07_nazca ===
Number four. The Nazca Lines, Peru.

In the desert of southern Peru, there are giant drawings on the ground: a hummingbird, a monkey, a spider, a whale, and straight lines that run for kilometers. Most are so big that you can only really see them from the air.

They were made by the Nazca people, roughly two thousand years ago, by moving dark stones aside to reveal the lighter ground below. In this dry, windless desert, they've survived ever since.

And we're still finding them. In 2024, researchers in Japan used artificial intelligence to scan the region and found three hundred and three new figures in just six months, almost doubling the number known. Why the Nazca made drawings they could never fully see from the ground is still one of archaeology's great questions. Paths for ceremonies, messages to the gods, maps of water? Scientists are still debating.

=== 08_baalbek ===
Number three. Baalbek, Lebanon.

In the Bekaa Valley of Lebanon stand the ruins of one of the largest temples the Romans ever built, the Temple of Jupiter. Under it, there are three limestone blocks known as the Trilithon. Each weighs around eight hundred tons, and they were lifted into a wall, several meters off the ground.

Nearby, in the ancient quarry, lie even bigger stones that never left. One, called the Stone of the Pregnant Woman, weighs about a thousand tons. In 2014, archaeologists dug beside it and found another block underneath, about twenty meters long, weighing an estimated sixteen hundred and fifty tons. That's about as heavy as four fully loaded jumbo jets.

It's the largest stone block ever carved by humans. It was probably left behind because it was simply too big to move. How the Romans moved the eight hundred ton blocks that did make it is still not fully understood.

=== 09_stonehenge ===
Number two. Stonehenge, England.

You know it. Everyone does. A ring of giant stones on Salisbury Plain, built in stages around five thousand years ago. And yet, the more scientists study it, the stranger it gets.

The giant sarsen stones came from about twenty-five kilometers away. The smaller bluestones were brought from the hills of west Wales, more than two hundred kilometers away. And then, in 2024, scientists made a discovery that shocked even them. The six-ton Altar Stone, lying at the heart of the monument, came from the far northeast of Scotland, at least seven hundred kilometers away.

Nobody knows how Stone Age people moved it that far, over mountains and rivers, or by sea. And nobody knows why they wanted that particular stone so badly. Five thousand years later, Stonehenge is still keeping its secrets.

=== 10_gobekli_tepe ===
Number one. Göbekli Tepe, Turkey.

On a hilltop in southeastern Turkey, archaeologists found circles of huge T-shaped stone pillars, some taller than five meters and weighing many tons, carved with foxes, boars, snakes, scorpions and vultures.

Then they dated it. Göbekli Tepe was built around eleven thousand six hundred years ago. That's about seven thousand years before the Great Pyramid, and six thousand years before Stonehenge. It was built before farming, before pottery, before writing, before the wheel.

For a long time, scientists believed people had to settle down and become farmers before they could build anything like this. Göbekli Tepe turned that idea upside down. Hunter-gatherers built it. Since the excavations began in 1995, every season has raised new questions. What was it for? Why was it built here? And who were the people who started building monuments before the world's first farms? We still don't know.
