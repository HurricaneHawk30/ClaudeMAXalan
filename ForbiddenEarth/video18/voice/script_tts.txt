=== 00_hook ===
Some places on Earth you will never be allowed to enter. Not with a ticket, not with a tour, not with a famous name. One hides hundreds of billions in gold under a busy street. One has been guarded by a single monk who will never leave it until he dies. One is a border so dangerous that nature took over. And at number one: a vault so secure that, in almost ninety years, outsiders have been let in only a handful of times.

=== 01_athos ===
Number fifteen. Mount [Athos](/ˈɑθɔs/), Greece.

In northern Greece, a long mountain peninsula sticks out into the [Aegean](/əʤˈiən/) Sea. On it stand twenty monasteries, some of them more than a thousand years old, clinging to cliffs above the water. Around two thousand monks live here, following a daily rhythm of prayer that has barely changed since the Middle Ages.

Mount [Athos](/ˈɑθɔs/) governs itself, and it has its own rules. For more than a thousand years, women have been banned from setting foot here, and boats carrying women must stay at least five hundred meters from the shore.

Even men can't just walk in. There are no roads from the mainland, so visitors arrive by boat, with a special permit. Each day, only a hundred Orthodox pilgrims and ten non-Orthodox visitors are allowed in, for a few days at most.

=== 02_daisen ===
Number fourteen. [Daisen](/dIsˈɛn/) [Kofun](/kˈOfun/), Japan.

In the middle of the city of [Sakai](/sɑkˈI/), near Osaka, there's a hill that isn't a hill. It's a giant tomb shaped like a keyhole, four hundred and eighty-six meters long and surrounded by three moats. By area, it's one of the largest tombs ever built, bigger than the base of the Great Pyramid.

It was built in the fifth century and is officially the tomb of Emperor [Nintoku](/nintˈOku/). But nobody knows for sure who lies inside, because nobody is allowed to look. The tomb belongs to the Imperial Household Agency, which forbids anyone from even standing on the mound. Archaeologists have only ever been allowed to study its outer edges.

Visitors can stand at a small gate by the moat and look at a wall of trees. To see the keyhole shape, you have to fly over it.

=== 03_lop_nur ===
Number thirteen. [Lop Nur](/lˈɑp nˈʊɹ/), China.

In the desert of western China lies a dried-out lake that, from space, looks like a giant human ear. Nearby is one of the most secret places in the country.

This was China's nuclear test site. On October sixteenth, nineteen sixty-four, China exploded its first atomic bomb here. Three years later, it tested its first hydrogen bomb. Over the next thirty-two years, there were forty-five nuclear tests at [Lop Nur](/lˈɑp nˈʊɹ/), the last one in July nineteen ninety-six.

The testing stopped, but the base didn't close. The area remains a military zone, closed to the public. Only two American nuclear weapons experts have ever officially visited. And in recent years, satellite images have shown new buildings and fresh tunnels being dug into the mountains. What they are for, China doesn't say.

=== 04_porton_down ===
Number twelve. [Porton](/pˈɔɹtən/) Down, England.

Near the quiet cathedral city of Salisbury, there's a site of around seven thousand acres surrounded by high fences and warning signs. On maps, the whole area is simply marked as a danger area.

[Porton](/pˈɔɹtən/) Down was set up in nineteen sixteen, after German forces used poison gas in the First World War. It has studied chemical and biological weapons ever since, and how to defend against them. During the Second World War, its scientists tested anthrax on [Gruinard](/ɡɹˈɪnjəɹd/) Island in Scotland, the island from the very first video on this channel.

Today it's home to Britain's Defence Science and Technology Laboratory. In twenty eighteen, its scientists identified the nerve agent used in an attack in nearby Salisbury. Thousands of people work here, and almost none of them can tell you exactly what they do.

=== 05_menwith_hill ===
Number eleven. R A F [Menwith](/mˈɛnwɪθ/) Hill, England.

Drive through the green hills of North Yorkshire and you'll see something strange: dozens of giant white balls sitting in a field, like enormous golf balls.

They're called radomes, and each one hides a satellite dish. [Menwith](/mˈɛnwɪθ/) Hill is officially a Royal Air Force station, but it's run largely by the United States, including the National Security Agency. It has been described as the largest electronic monitoring station in the world.

The base has been here since the nineteen fifties. For decades, very little about it was public, until documents leaked over the years showed it collecting huge amounts of communications from across the globe. Protesters have camped outside the fence. But the gates stay shut, and the golf balls keep listening.

=== 06_pine_gap ===
Number ten. Pine Gap, Australia.

In the heart of the Australian outback, about eighteen kilometers from the town of Alice Springs, a road leads past "prohibited area" signs to a cluster of white domes in the desert.

This is Pine Gap, a joint Australian and American intelligence base set up under a treaty in nineteen sixty-six. It controls and receives data from spy satellites high above the Earth, and it can detect missile launches on the other side of the planet.

Hundreds of people work here, but the public knows very little about what they do. The air above the base is restricted. Some Australians call it their own Area fifty-one. It sits so far from anywhere that the nearest big city is more than a thousand kilometers away.

=== 07_diego_garcia ===
Number nine. [Diego Garcia](/diˈAɡO ɡɑɹsˈiə/), Indian Ocean.

From the air, [Diego Garcia](/diˈAɡO ɡɑɹsˈiə/) looks like a tropical paradise: a thin ring of white sand and palm trees around a turquoise lagoon, in the middle of the Indian Ocean.

But you can't visit. Since the nineteen seventies, it has been one of the most important military bases of the United States and Britain, with a runway long enough for heavy bombers. To build it, Britain removed the islanders who lived across the [Chagos](/ʧˈɑɡOs/) Islands, between one and two thousand people, a decision that is still bitterly disputed today.

In May twenty twenty-five, Britain signed a treaty to hand the [Chagos](/ʧˈɑɡOs/) Islands to [Mauritius](/məɹˈɪʃəs/), while keeping the base on [Diego Garcia](/diˈAɡO ɡɑɹsˈiə/) under a lease of at least ninety-nine years. As of late twenty twenty-six, the treaty still hadn't come into force. Either way, [Diego Garcia](/diˈAɡO ɡɑɹsˈiə/) stays closed to almost everyone.

=== 08_great_taboo ===
Number eight. The Great Taboo, Mongolia.

When [Genghis](/ʤˈɛŋɡɪs/) Khan died in twelve twenty-seven, he was buried in secret. Legends say that soldiers rode horses over the grave to erase every trace.

What we know is this: after his death, an area of about two hundred and forty square kilometers around the sacred mountain of [Burkhan Khaldun](/buɹkˈɑn kɑldˈun/) was declared a forbidden zone, the [Ikh Khorig](/ˈik kˈɔɹɪɡ/), or Great Taboo. It was guarded by a clan of warriors, and anyone who entered without permission could be put to death.

The ban lasted for centuries. Even in the twentieth century, the area stayed closed. Today, it lies within a strictly protected area and a [Unesco](/junˈɛskO/) World Heritage Site. [Genghis](/ʤˈɛŋɡɪs/) Khan's tomb has never been found, and many Mongolians would prefer that it never is.

=== 09_metro2 ===
Number seven. Metro two, Russia.

Moscow's metro is famous for its palace-like stations, with chandeliers and marble halls. But for decades, there have been stories of a second metro, far deeper, that doesn't appear on any map.

It's known as Metro two. According to these accounts, it was started under Stalin, and its secret lines connect the Kremlin with government bunkers, military sites and an airport, so that leaders could escape during a nuclear war. In nineteen ninety-one, a U S Defense Department report even published a drawing of what it believed were secret lines under Moscow.

Russian officials have never confirmed that Metro two exists. They've never denied it either. Urban explorers say they've seen strange tunnels and sealed doors. But nobody outside a small circle of people has ever officially been allowed to ride it.

=== 10_yamantau ===
Number six. Mount [Yamantau](/jˌɑmɑntˈɑu/), Russia.

In the southern Ural Mountains, there's a mountain called [Yamantau](/jˌɑmɑntˈɑu/). Its name in the local language is often translated as "evil mountain." At its foot lies [Mezhgorye](/mɛʒɡˈɔɹjə/), a closed town that doesn't welcome outsiders.

In the nineteen nineties, American satellites spotted huge construction work at the mountain, with tunnels being dug deep inside it. U S officials believed it was a giant underground complex, possibly a bunker for Russia's leaders during a nuclear war.

When asked, Russian officials gave several different answers over the years, from a mining site to a store for food and treasures. To this day, no outsider has been allowed to see what's inside the mountain, and the town of [Mezhgorye](/mɛʒɡˈɔɹjə/) is still closed.

=== 11_vatican_archive ===
Number five. The Vatican Apostolic Archive, Vatican City.

Deep under the Vatican, there's a two-story underground vault, nicknamed the Bunker. It's part of the archive of the popes, which holds more than a thousand years of history on about eighty-five kilometers of shelves.

Among its documents are letters about King Henry the Eighth's request to divorce, and records from the trial of Galileo. Until twenty nineteen, it was officially called the Vatican Secret Archive. Pope Francis changed the name, saying "secret" was misunderstood. It meant "private," the personal archive of the pope.

But it's still not open to the public. Only qualified researchers can apply, and they can't wander the shelves. They request specific documents, and only from periods the Vatican has opened, usually decades after a pope's reign ends.

=== 12_bank_of_england ===
Number four. The gold vaults of the Bank of England.

Every day, thousands of people walk along [Threadneedle](/θɹˈɛdnˌidəl/) Street in the City of London. Few think about what's under their feet: one of the largest stores of gold in the world.

The Bank of England's vaults hold around four hundred thousand gold bars, each weighing about twelve and a half kilograms. That's thousands of tons of gold, worth hundreds of billions. Most of it doesn't even belong to Britain. It's kept here for other countries' central banks. Only the Federal Reserve Bank of New York is believed to hold more.

The vaults were built in the nineteen thirties, and they're protected by modern security systems, including voice recognition. But some of the doors are still opened with giant keys almost a meter long.

=== 13_chapel_of_the_tablet ===
Number three. The Chapel of the Tablet, Ethiopia.

In the ancient city of [Aksum](/ˈɑksum/), in northern Ethiopia, stands a small chapel behind an iron fence. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church believes that inside it lies the Ark of the Covenant, the chest that held the stone tablets of the Ten Commandments.

Only one person on Earth is allowed to see it: the guardian monk. He is chosen for life by the guardian before him. Once he takes the role, he never leaves the grounds of the chapel. He prays, burns incense, and guards the Ark until he dies. Not even the head of the Ethiopian Church may enter.

Is it really the Ark? No outsider has ever been able to check. Visitors can only look at the chapel from beyond the fence, and the guardian keeps his secret.

=== 14_korean_dmz ===
Number two. The Korean Demilitarized Zone.

In nineteen fifty-three, the fighting in the Korean War stopped, but the war never officially ended. Instead, a strip of land was drawn across the peninsula: about two hundred and fifty kilometers long and four kilometers wide. It's called the Demilitarized Zone, but it's one of the most heavily armed borders on Earth.

On both sides there are fences, guard posts and thousands of soldiers. The zone itself is thought to hold up to two million landmines. Almost no one has walked through its center for more than seventy years.

And something unexpected happened. Without people, nature took over. Forests and wetlands grew back, and rare animals like red-crowned cranes now live in the most dangerous strip of land in Asia.

=== 15_fort_knox ===
Number one. Fort Knox, USA.

In Kentucky, inside a huge army base, stands a two-story building of granite, concrete and steel. It's the United States Bullion Depository, better known as Fort Knox.

It was finished in nineteen thirty-six, and the first gold arrived the next year, sent by U S Mail. Today it holds about a hundred and forty-seven million ounces of gold, roughly half of all the gold owned by the U S Treasury. During World War Two, it also guarded the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution.

The vault door is said to weigh more than twenty tons, and no single person knows the whole combination. Visitors are simply not permitted. In almost ninety years, outsiders have been let in only a handful of times: President Roosevelt in nineteen forty-three, a group of journalists and members of Congress in nineteen seventy-four, and a small group of officials in twenty seventeen. In twenty twenty-five, calls to audit the gold made headlines again. The Treasury says it's all there. You'll just have to take its word for it.
