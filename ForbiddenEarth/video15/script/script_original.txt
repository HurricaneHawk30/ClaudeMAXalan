=== 00_hook ===
Most volcanoes sleep for thousands of years. These ten don't. One of them dropped ash on one of the biggest cities in the world this year. One drained a lake of lava in less than an hour. One split a town in two and sent all its people away in a single night. And at number one: a volcano so wide and so flat that half a million people live inside it, and the ground under their homes is rising right now.

=== 01_fuji ===
Number ten. Mount Fuji, Japan.

It's the most famous mountain in Japan, an almost perfect cone, three thousand seven hundred and seventy-six meters tall. On a clear day, you can see it from Tokyo, about a hundred kilometers away. It's easy to forget that it's an active volcano.

Fuji last erupted in 1707, just forty-nine days after one of the strongest earthquakes in Japan's history. For about sixteen days it threw ash into the sky, and that ash fell on Edo, the city we now call Tokyo.

Today, more than thirty-seven million people live in the Tokyo region. In 2025, Japan's government issued new guidelines for a Fuji eruption, warning that ash could stop trains, cut power and block roads across the capital within days. Planners estimate that an eruption like the one in 1707 could leave around four hundred and ninety million cubic meters of ash across the region. Before 1707, Fuji erupted far more often. Scientists say its long silence doesn't mean it's finished.

=== 02_rainier ===
Number nine. Mount Rainier, USA.

Mount Rainier towers over the Seattle area at four thousand three hundred and ninety-two meters, and it carries more glacier ice than any other peak in the lower forty-eight states. That ice is the problem.

When a volcano like this wakes up, it can melt its glaciers fast. And its weak, rotten rock can simply collapse. Either way, the result is a lahar: a river of mud, rock and broken trees that can travel tens of kilometers down a valley in about an hour. Around five hundred years ago, one of these, the Electron Mudflow, reached the lowlands where towns like Orting stand today.

In 2018, the U.S. Geological Survey ranked Rainier the third most dangerous volcano in the country. Around eighty thousand people live in areas that lahars could reach. Since 1998, sensors have been watching the valleys. In the towns below, warning sirens are tested, and schoolchildren practice walking to high ground. In some places, they'd have less than an hour.

=== 03_taal ===
Number eight. Taal Volcano, the Philippines.

Taal is small. Its island rises only about three hundred meters out of a lake on the island of Luzon, around sixty kilometers south of Manila. But it's one of the most active volcanoes in the Philippines, and one of the deadliest.

In 1911, an eruption here killed more than thirteen hundred people. In January 2020, Taal woke up again, blasting ash more than fifteen kilometers into the sky. Lightning flashed inside the cloud, ash fell on Manila, and hundreds of thousands of people had to leave their homes.

The island itself is now a Permanent Danger Zone, where nobody is allowed to live. But Taal hasn't calmed down. It had a string of small eruptions through 2026, and in October it sent up another column of ash about four kilometers high. And all around the lake lies one of the most crowded regions of the country.

=== 04_etna ===
Number seven. Mount Etna, Italy.

Etna is Europe's most active volcano, rising more than three thousand meters above the island of Sicily. It erupts so often that in Catania, the city at its foot, people sweep black ash off their balconies the way other people clear snow.

In August 2026, Etna erupted from one of its summit craters, the Voragine. Ash and fountains of lava forced Catania's airport to close again and again for about a week. Around seven hundred flights were cancelled, and around a hundred thousand travelers were stranded in the middle of the summer holidays. The ash cloud drifted as far as Malta and Libya.

Then, at the end of September, Etna erupted again. It almost never stays quiet for long. Scientists watch it every hour of every day, because Catania and dozens of towns are built right on its slopes.

=== 05_nyiragongo ===
Number six. Mount Nyiragongo, Democratic Republic of the Congo.

For decades, Nyiragongo held one of the largest lava lakes on Earth, a boiling pool of molten rock inside its crater. And its lava is unusual. It's so runny that it moves almost like water.

In 1977, the crater walls cracked, and the lava lake drained in less than an hour. The lava raced down the slopes at up to sixty kilometers an hour, the fastest lava flow ever recorded. In 2002, lava poured straight into the city of Goma, through its streets and across part of its airport runway. And in May 2021, it erupted again with almost no warning. The lava stopped just short of the city, but at least thirty-two people died, and hundreds of thousands fled.

Goma, home to more than a million people, lies only about fifteen kilometers from the crater. Every eruption here is a race against time.

=== 06_sakurajima ===
Number five. Sakurajima, Japan.

Across the bay from the city of Kagoshima, home to about half a million people, Sakurajima erupts all the time. Small explosions happen many times a year, sometimes hundreds of times, throwing ash high over the water.

People here live with it. The city hands out special bags for collecting volcanic ash. Weather reports tell you which way the ash will blow. And on the volcano itself, children wear helmets on their way to school.

But it hasn't always been small explosions. In 1914, Sakurajima had the biggest eruption in twentieth-century Japan. So much lava poured out that the island became joined to the mainland. Fifty-eight people died. Today, the volcano is on alert level three, which means: do not approach. And scientists who measure the ground say magma has been building up under the bay again for more than a century.

=== 07_reykjanes ===
Number four. The Reykjanes Peninsula, Iceland.

For about eight hundred years, the volcanoes of southwest Iceland were quiet. Then, in 2021, they woke up. And in November 2023, the ground under the fishing town of Grindavík split open. Hundreds of earthquakes shook the town, streets cracked in two, and all of its people, around thirty-seven hundred of them, were evacuated in a single night.

Since then, the crater row next to Grindavík has erupted again and again, nine times between December 2023 and the summer of 2025. In January 2024, lava crossed the town's defenses and burned several homes. The famous Blue Lagoon had to be evacuated many times. Iceland built huge earth walls to steer the lava away from the town and from the Svartsengi power plant.

Then the eruptions paused. But underground, magma has kept flowing back into the chamber beneath Svartsengi. Iceland's Met Office says another eruption is likely, and it could start with less than an hour's warning. Most of Grindavík's people still haven't moved back home.

=== 08_merapi ===
Number three. Mount Merapi, Indonesia.

Its name means "Mountain of Fire," and it lives up to it. Merapi, on the island of Java, is the most active volcano in Indonesia, with small eruptions every few years. Millions of people live within sight of it, including the city of Yogyakarta, less than thirty kilometers away.

Merapi's deadliest weapon is the pyroclastic flow: a glowing avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock that can rush down its slopes at well over a hundred kilometers an hour. Nothing outruns it.

In 2010, Merapi had its biggest eruption in more than a century. Pyroclastic flows swept down the valleys, more than three hundred and fifty people died, and around three hundred and fifty thousand were evacuated. Since November 2020, the volcano has been on alert level three out of four. A lava dome keeps growing at its summit, and hot avalanches keep rolling down its slopes.

=== 09_popocatepetl ===
Number two. Popocatépetl, Mexico.

In the old Aztec language, its name means "Smoking Mountain." Mexicans just call it "El Popo." At more than five thousand four hundred meters, it's the second-highest peak in Mexico, and it stands about seventy kilometers from Mexico City. Around twenty-five million people live within a hundred kilometers of its crater.

After decades of quiet, Popocatépetl woke up in 1994, and it has been erupting on and off ever since, puffing out steam, gas and ash almost every day. In 2023, falling ash shut down the airports of Mexico City and Puebla for hours, and schools around the volcano were closed. In February 2026, ash fell on Mexico City again.

The alert level is yellow, and nobody is allowed within twelve kilometers of the crater. Scientists watch it around the clock, ready to call for evacuations if it turns from noisy to violent.

=== 10_campi_flegrei ===
Number one. Campi Flegrei, Italy.

You probably know Vesuvius, the volcano that buried Pompeii. But just west of Naples, there is another volcano, much bigger and much harder to see. Campi Flegrei, the "Burning Fields," is not a mountain. It's a caldera, a giant collapsed crater about fifteen kilometers wide, and part of the Naples area is built right inside it.

About thirty-nine thousand years ago, it produced the largest eruption in Europe in the last two hundred thousand years. Its last eruption was much smaller. In 1538, after years of earthquakes and rising ground, the earth opened near the town of Pozzuoli and built a brand-new hill in about a week. It's still there. It's called Monte Nuovo, "the New Mountain."

Now the ground is rising again. Since 2005, the center of Pozzuoli has risen by nearly one and a half meters, recently by about a centimeter a month. Earthquakes shake the area more and more often, and on July 31, 2026, the strongest one since modern monitoring began hit with a magnitude of four point seven. About half a million people live in the official red zone, the area that would have to be evacuated first.

Scientists stress that rising ground doesn't mean an eruption is certain, or coming soon. But Campi Flegrei is the volcano that experts watch most closely in Europe. Because if it ever does erupt, it won't be on a mountain far away. It will be under a city.
