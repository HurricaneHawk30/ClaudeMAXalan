=== 00_hook ===
Most towns die slowly. People move away one by one, shops close, and the grass grows back over decades. But some towns died almost overnight. A storm, a flood, a volcano, an order from the army, or an invisible poison, and suddenly, everyone was gone. Plates left on tables. Toys left on floors. These are ten towns that were abandoned almost overnight. And at number one: a city of nearly fifty thousand people who were told they'd be back in three days.

=== 01_kennecott ===
Number ten. Kennecott, Alaska.

Deep in the mountains of Alaska, in what is now Wrangell–St. Elias National Park, stand the red wooden buildings of a copper town. In the early 1900s, prospectors found one of the richest copper deposits ever discovered here, and a railway nearly two hundred miles long was built through the wilderness to carry the ore to the coast.

From 1911, Kennecott was a busy company town, with a hospital, a school, a store and a giant mill climbing the side of the mountain. But by 1938, the best ore was gone. The mines closed, the last train left, and the workers went with it. Much of the town was simply left behind.

For decades, it stood empty in the cold. Today, the National Park Service looks after it, and visitors can walk among the old buildings, still painted red.

=== 02_pyramiden ===
Number nine. Pyramiden, Svalbard.

On a Norwegian archipelago, about thirteen hundred kilometers from the North Pole, there's a Soviet town. Pyramiden was founded by Sweden in 1910 and sold to the Soviet Union in 1927. It became a model mining settlement, with apartment blocks, a cultural center, the world's northernmost swimming pool and the northernmost monument to Vladimir Lenin, still staring across the main square.

In 1998, the coal mine was no longer worth running. The last coal came out on March 31st. By October, the last permanent resident was gone. People left furniture, books and pictures behind, and the Arctic cold has preserved much of it ever since.

For years, Pyramiden was completely empty. Today, a handful of workers live there in summer, and tourists arrive by boat to walk through a Soviet town frozen in 1998.

=== 03_hashima ===
Number eight. Hashima Island, Japan.

Off the coast of Nagasaki, there's an island that looks like a warship. It's covered with concrete apartment blocks, surrounded by a high sea wall, and that's why it's nicknamed Gunkanjima, Battleship Island. Under it lay an undersea coal mine.

In 1959, more than five thousand people lived on an island of about six hectares. It was one of the most densely populated places ever recorded. There's also a dark side to its history: during the Second World War, Korean and Chinese workers were forced to work in its mines under harsh conditions.

In January 1974, with the coal running out, Mitsubishi closed the mine. By April 20th, every resident had left. The island stood sealed and empty for thirty-five years, until tourists were allowed back in 2009. In 2015, it became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

=== 04_hallsands ===
Number seven. Hallsands, England.

On the coast of Devon, there was a fishing village built on a rock ledge just above the beach. It was protected by a thick bank of shingle, the small stones that took the force of the waves. Then, from 1897, contractors began dredging that shingle from the bay, to build the new naval dockyard at nearby Devonport.

Without its natural shield, the beach began to drop. Storms started hitting the houses. And on the night of January 26th, 1917, a storm and a high tide arrived together. Waves smashed through walls and roofs. By midnight, the village was destroyed. Amazingly, every villager survived.

Hallsands was never rebuilt. Today, you can look down from a viewing platform at the ruins of the houses, still clinging to the rocks, with the sea that took them washing below.

=== 05_tyneham ===
Number six. Tyneham, England.

In Dorset, in a quiet valley near the sea, stood a small village with a church, a school and a manor house. In late 1943, the British army needed land to train for the invasion of Europe. About 225 people were told to leave, just before Christmas.

Before they went, someone pinned a note to the church door. It asked soldiers to treat the houses with care, and ended with a promise: We shall return one day.

They never did. After the war, the army kept the land, and in 1948 it was bought for good. Tyneham is still inside a military firing range. The houses are roofless shells, but the church and the school have been preserved. On many weekends, when the guns are silent, visitors can walk through a village where time stopped in 1943.

=== 06_chaiten ===
Number five. Chaitén, Chile.

In southern Chile, the small port town of Chaitén sat at the foot of a volcano that most people thought was dead. On May 2nd, 2008, it woke up for the first time in thousands of years. Ash poured out of the sky. Within days, the whole town of several thousand people had been evacuated, mostly by sea.

Then came the second disaster. Heavy rain washed the ash down the mountains, and the Blanco River filled with mud and debris. The river burst out of its bed and changed course, straight through the middle of town. Houses were buried up to their windows in grey mud.

Many people thought Chaitén was finished. But some residents refused to give up on it. They came back, cleaned out their homes, and slowly, part of the town came back to life, beside a volcano that is still being watched.

=== 07_times_beach ===
Number four. Times Beach, Missouri.

Times Beach was a small town by the Meramec River, about twenty miles from St. Louis. In the 1970s, to keep the dust down on its dirt roads, the town paid a contractor to spray them with waste oil. Nobody knew that the oil was contaminated with dioxin, one of the most toxic chemicals ever made.

In December 1982, the river flooded the town, and dioxin was found in the soil. In February 1983, the Environmental Protection Agency announced that the whole town would be bought out and every resident permanently relocated. It cost around thirty million dollars. In 1985, Times Beach stopped existing as a town.

The houses were demolished and buried, and the contaminated soil was burned. Today, the land is Route 66 State Park. Visitors walk and cycle where the streets used to be, and almost nothing shows that a town was ever here.

=== 08_epecuen ===
Number three. Villa Epecuén, Argentina.

In the Argentine pampas, on the shore of a very salty lake, there was a spa town. Thousands of tourists came every summer to float in the lake and bathe in its waters, believed to heal the body. There were hotels, shops and restaurants for more than a thousand residents.

Then, in November 1985, after a long run of heavy rains, the dam that protected the town broke. The lake poured into the streets. People fled with what they could carry, and the water kept rising. By the early 1990s, the town lay under about ten meters of salt water.

It stayed underwater for more than twenty years. When the water finally pulled back, around 2009, it revealed a grey, ghostly town: dead trees bleached white by the salt, rusted cars, and streets full of broken walls. For years, its population was just one man, who came back to live alone among the ruins.

=== 09_futaba ===
Number two. Futaba, Japan.

On March 11th, 2011, a massive earthquake and tsunami struck Japan, and the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant went into meltdown. Part of the plant stood in the town of Futaba. The next day, its roughly seven thousand residents were ordered to leave. Most thought they'd be gone for a few days.

For years, Futaba was frozen. Shop shelves stayed stocked, bicycles stayed parked, and over the main road hung a sign that had greeted visitors for decades. It read: Nuclear power, energy for a bright future. It was eventually taken down.

It took eleven years before anyone was allowed to live in Futaba again. In 2022, the evacuation order was lifted for a small part of the town. Only a small number of people have come back so far, to a place that once held thousands.

=== 10_pripyat ===
Number one. Pripyat, Ukraine.

Pripyat was founded in 1970, a model Soviet city for the workers of the nearby Chernobyl nuclear power plant. It was young and modern. By 1986, almost fifty thousand people lived here, in new apartment blocks, with schools, a stadium, a cinema and a swimming pool.

In the early hours of April 26th, 1986, reactor number four exploded. For more than a day, life in Pripyat went on almost as normal. Then, on the afternoon of April 27th, around twelve hundred buses arrived. Residents were told to take only what they needed, because they would be back in three days. In about three and a half hours, the whole city was empty.

Nobody ever came back to live here. In the center of town stands a yellow Ferris wheel, part of an amusement park that was supposed to open on May 1st. Decades later, it still stands there, waiting for a crowd that will never come.
