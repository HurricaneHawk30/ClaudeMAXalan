=== 00_hook ===
Some places are so beautiful that people forget to be afraid. A beach in Hawaii with a sign that counts the dead. A swimming pool on the very edge of a giant waterfall. A tree that seems to grow apples. These ten places draw visitors from all over the world, and every one of them has a way to kill you. And at number one: a blue hole in the Red Sea, where divers keep chasing an underwater arch that has already taken dozens of lives.

=== 01_manchineel ===
Number ten. The Manchineel Tree, the Caribbean.

On beaches from Florida to Colombia, there's a tree that looks perfect for a lazy afternoon. Wide green leaves, plenty of shade, and small fruit that looks like green apples and smells sweet. The Spanish called it the little apple of death.

Every part of the manchineel is poisonous. Its milky sap blisters the skin. Stand under it in the rain, and the water dripping from its leaves can burn you. Burn its wood, and the smoke can hurt your eyes. In 1999, a British doctor on holiday in Tobago took one small bite of the fruit. Within minutes, her mouth was burning, and for hours she could barely swallow.

Some accounts say the arrow that killed the explorer Juan Ponce de León in 1521 was tipped with manchineel sap.

Today, many of these trees carry a red band or a red X on the trunk. It means one thing: don't even sit here.

=== 02_natron ===
Number nine. Lake Natron, Tanzania.

In northern Tanzania, beneath an active volcano, lies a lake that can turn blood red. The color comes from tiny salt-loving microbes that bloom in its water.

The lake is fed by hot springs and volcanic minerals. Its water can be almost as caustic as ammonia, and in the shallows it can reach sixty degrees Celsius. It burns the skin and eyes of animals that aren't adapted to it. Birds and bats that die here don't rot the usual way. The minerals dry them out and coat them in a hard crust, until they look like they've been turned to stone.

And yet, this deadly lake is also a nursery. About three quarters of all the lesser flamingos on Earth hatch here, on salt islands in the middle of the lake, where predators can't follow.

=== 03_hanakapiai ===
Number eight. Hanakapiai Beach, Hawaii.

On the island of Kauai, a trail winds along the Na Pali Coast, past green cliffs that drop straight into the Pacific. After two miles of mud and jungle, hikers reach a perfect little beach. No road. No lifeguard. No crowds.

Before the sand, there's a sign. It says: do not go near the water. Unseen currents have killed. And instead of a number, there are rows of hand-scratched tally marks, one for every person said to have drowned here. By 2014, there were more than eighty.

The sign has no official author, and researchers count fewer deaths, around thirty since 1970. But the danger is real. There's no reef to break the waves, so the full force of the ocean hits the shore, and a rip current can pull a swimmer far out to sea. The nearest safe place to land is miles away.

=== 04_hua ===
Number seven. Mount Hua, China.

Mount Hua is one of China's five sacred mountains: Taoist temples, twisted pines, and granite peaks that rise straight out of the clouds. For centuries, monks and pilgrims climbed it to find peace.

Its most famous path is the Plank Walk. On the South Peak, more than two thousand meters up, a line of wooden boards is pinned to a sheer cliff with iron spikes. In places, it's only about thirty centimeters wide. Below it, there's nothing but air.

The first path here is said to have been built about seven hundred years ago, by a Taoist master who wanted to meditate far away from the world. Today, visitors wear a harness, clip it to a chain bolted to the rock, and shuffle sideways with their faces to the stone.

Rumors say many people have died on this mountain over the years. No official numbers have ever been published.

=== 05_devils_pool ===
Number six. Devil's Pool, Zambia.

Victoria Falls, on the border of Zambia and Zimbabwe, is one of the largest waterfalls on Earth. In the rainy season, more than five hundred million liters of water pour over its edge every minute, and the spray rises so high that locals call it the smoke that thunders.

But for a few months each year, when the Zambezi River runs low, something strange appears. Right at the lip of the falls, next to Livingstone Island, there's a natural rock pool. Visitors swim out to it, then lean over the edge of a drop more than a hundred meters high.

An underwater rock wall stops the current from carrying them over. Guides often hold swimmers by the legs while they take the photo.

Only licensed guides can bring people here, and only when the water is low enough. When the river rises again, the pool disappears, and the whole Zambezi goes over that edge.

=== 06_ice_caves ===
Number five. The Ice Caves of Iceland.

Under Vatnajökull, Iceland's largest ice cap, meltwater carves tunnels through the glaciers. Inside, the ice glows in every shade of blue, with walls so clear you can see air bubbles trapped for hundreds of years.

But ice caves are not permanent. They change every season, and some vanish completely. In winter, the ice is hard and stable. In summer, the walls and roofs can melt by several centimeters a day.

In 2017, scientists warned that summer ice cave tours carry a very high risk. Then, in August 2024, a tour group was visiting an ice cave on a glacier in southeastern Iceland. An ice arch above them collapsed. One visitor was killed, and another was badly hurt.

Tours were stopped while the accident was investigated, and Iceland began working on stricter rules. The caves are still there. Still beautiful. And still melting.

=== 07_antelope ===
Number four. Antelope Canyon, Arizona.

On Navajo land near the town of Page, there are cracks in the desert so narrow you could walk right past them. Step inside, and the red sandstone curves around you like waves frozen in stone. At midday, beams of sunlight fall through the gaps and light up the dust.

These shapes were carved by water. When storms hit the desert, flash floods rush through the canyon and polish its walls.

On August 12, 1997, the sky above Lower Antelope Canyon looked calm. But miles upstream, a thunderstorm had dumped heavy rain. A wall of water rushed into the canyon. Eleven tourists were killed. Only their guide survived.

Today, the canyon can only be visited with a Navajo-licensed guide. Metal stairs are bolted to the walls, and tours are cancelled whenever there's a risk of flooding.

=== 08_jacobs_well ===
Number three. Jacob's Well, Texas.

In the Texas Hill Country, near the small town of Wimberley, a creek runs over flat limestone. In the middle of it is a round hole about four meters wide, full of water so clear and blue it looks like a window into the earth. An early settler said it looked like a well from Bible times, and the name stuck.

In summer, swimmers jump into it from the rocks. But the hole doesn't end. It drops about nine meters straight down, then turns into an underwater cave that goes deeper, through a chain of chambers joined by narrow passages full of loose gravel.

At least eight divers have died here. In 1980, after the way into the deeper chambers was blocked with a metal grate, divers tore it open and left a note on a plastic slate: You can't keep us out.

Today, only a team of trained cave divers is allowed to go down.

=== 09_yellowstone ===
Number two. The Hot Springs of Yellowstone, USA.

Yellowstone National Park sits on top of a supervolcano, and it has more geysers than anywhere else on Earth. Its most famous hot spring, Grand Prismatic, is wider than a football field is long. Its center is deep blue, ringed with bands of green, yellow and orange, painted by heat-loving microbes.

It looks like you could swim in it. You can't. Many of Yellowstone's springs are close to boiling. The ground around them can be a thin crust over scalding water, and one step off the boardwalk can break through.

Bears get the headlines, but bears have killed about eight people in Yellowstone's history. Hot springs have killed more than twenty. That's why the park's first rule in thermal areas is simple: stay on the boardwalk.

=== 10_blue_hole ===
Number one. The Blue Hole, Egypt.

On the coast of the Sinai Peninsula, near the town of Dahab, the coral reef suddenly opens into a circle of deep blue. The Blue Hole is a sinkhole more than a hundred meters deep, surrounded by bright coral and fish. Snorkelers float over its edge every day.

What draws divers is hidden far below. At about fifty-five meters, a tunnel called the Arch cuts through the reef wall and leads out to the open sea. In the clear water, it looks closer and shorter than it really is. It's about twenty-six meters long.

At that depth, breathing ordinary air causes nitrogen narcosis. Divers feel drunk, lose track of time and depth, and make fatal mistakes. Egyptian authorities have counted around forty deaths here. Other estimates run much higher.

On the rocks above the water, families have left memorial plaques. Divers walk past them on the way in. And many still go looking for the Arch.
