=== 00_hook ===
When people leave, nature doesn't wait. A ship that turned into a floating forest. A church with trees growing out of its roof. Henry Ford's American dream town, lost in the Amazon. And at number one: a whole village that disappeared under a blanket of green.

=== 01_bannerman ===
Number ten. Bannerman's Castle, USA.

On a tiny island in the Hudson River, about eighty kilometers north of New York City, stand the ruins of what looks like a Scottish castle. It isn't one. It was a warehouse for weapons.

Francis Bannerman was the world's biggest dealer in old military equipment. In 1900, he bought the island to store tons of gunpowder and shells safely away from the city, and built a castle-style arsenal with his own name written across the wall.

In 1920, two hundred tons of shells and powder exploded and blew part of the complex apart. The family slowly left. In 1969, a fire burned out the roofs and floors. Trees and vines moved into the empty shell. Today, volunteers hold the walls together with steel, and boats bring visitors to see a castle that nature is slowly pulling down.

=== 02_ayrfield ===
Number nine. The SS Ayrfield, Australia.

In Homebush Bay, in Sydney, there's a ship covered in trees. The SS Ayrfield was built in Scotland in 1911, a steel cargo ship more than seventy meters long. During the Second World War, it carried supplies to American troops in the Pacific. Later, it hauled coal along the coast for decades.

In 1972, it was brought to Homebush Bay to be broken up for scrap. But the scrapyard closed before the job was done, and the Ayrfield was simply left behind.

Seeds from the nearby mangroves drifted into its rusty hull. They took root, and kept growing. Today, a whole forest of mangrove trees grows out of the ship, spilling over its sides. Locals call it the Floating Forest.

=== 03_kayakoy ===
Number eight. Kayaköy, Turkey.

On a hillside in southwest Turkey, hundreds of roofless stone houses climb up the slope, side by side. This was Levissi, a town where Greek Orthodox Christians lived for centuries next to their Muslim Turkish neighbors.

In 1923, after a war between Greece and Turkey, the two countries agreed to a forced population exchange. The Christians of Levissi, thousands of them, had to leave for Greece. Muslims arriving from Greece were meant to take their place, but most didn't stay. The town was renamed Kayaköy, which means "rock village," and it slowly emptied.

An earthquake in 1957 damaged the abandoned houses even more. Today, fig and pine trees grow between the walls and inside the empty rooms. It's preserved as a museum village, a quiet reminder of the people who had to go.

=== 04_city_methodist ===
Number seven. City Methodist Church, USA.

In 1926, the steel city of Gary, Indiana, was booming, and it opened one of the grandest churches in the Midwest. City Methodist Church cost eight hundred thousand dollars, had a theater, a gym and room for thousands of people.

But as the steel industry declined, people left Gary, and the congregation shrank. The church closed in 1975.

Since then, rain and snow have poured through the broken roof. Trees now grow on its upper levels, and plants creep across the floors of the great hall where the pews once stood. It has become one of the most photographed ruins in America, and a filming location for movies. In 2026, plans were announced to demolish it. Soon, it may be gone for good.

=== 05_ross_island ===
Number six. Ross Island, India.

Off the coast of the Andaman Islands, in the Bay of Bengal, there's a small island once called the Paris of the East. In the 1800s, it was the headquarters of the British in the Andamans, with grand houses, a church, a bakery, a swimming pool and a club for officers. It was also the headquarters of a brutal penal colony.

In 1941, an earthquake damaged the island. In 1942, Japanese forces occupied it during the Second World War. After the war, the British never really came back.

Then the trees moved in. Today, giant fig roots wrap around the walls of the church and the old government buildings like snakes, holding the ruins together and tearing them apart at the same time. Deer and peacocks walk freely among them. In 2018, India renamed the island after the independence leader Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose.

=== 06_fordlandia ===
Number five. Fordlândia, Brazil.

In 1928, Henry Ford, one of the richest men in the world, had a problem. His cars needed rubber, and other countries controlled most of it. So he decided to grow his own, in the Amazon rainforest.

He shipped a whole American town to Brazil: a water tower, a hospital, a sawmill, neat houses with porches. He called it Fordlândia. Managers tried to make the workers live like Americans, from the food they ate to the dances they went to.

It was a disaster. Ford hadn't consulted experts on growing rubber. The trees, planted close together, were destroyed by disease and pests. Workers rioted. In 1945, the company sold the land back to Brazil, losing more than twenty million dollars. Today, the jungle has crept back over the factory and many of the empty houses. People still live in Fordlândia, among the ruins of Henry Ford's dream.

=== 07_craco ===
Number four. Craco, Italy.

In southern Italy, a medieval town sits on top of a steep hill, with a tall stone tower at its peak. For almost a thousand years, people lived here. Today, almost no one does.

In 1963, a series of landslides began to pull the ground out from under the town, possibly made worse by new water and sewage pipes. Craco's last residents, around eighteen hundred people, were moved to a new village in the valley below. A flood and an earthquake in the following years finished the job.

Now, plants grow through the cracks of empty churches, and birds nest in the houses. The town has become a favorite of filmmakers, appearing in movies including a James Bond film. Visitors can explore it, but only on guided tours, wearing a helmet.

=== 08_kolmanskop ===
Number three. Kolmanskop, Namibia.

In 1908, a railway worker in the Namib Desert found a shiny stone in the sand. It was a diamond. Within a few years, German miners built a rich town on the spot, with a ballroom, a casino, a theater, a bowling alley and a hospital with the first X-ray machine in the southern hemisphere.

But the diamonds near the town ran out, and richer fields were found further south. People started to leave in the 1930s. By 1956, Kolmanskop was empty.

Then the desert took over. Wind pushed the sand through doors and windows. Today, dunes fill the elegant rooms up to the windowsills, and bathtubs are full of sand instead of water. It's one of the strangest places on Earth: a German town, slowly drowning in the desert.

=== 09_ta_prohm ===
Number two. Ta Prohm, Cambodia.

In the jungle near Angkor, there's a temple where giant trees grow right on top of the stone. Their roots pour over the walls and doorways like melted wax, splitting the stones and holding them together at the same time.

Ta Prohm was built in the late twelfth century by King Jayavarman the Seventh, as a Buddhist monastery. Inscriptions say thousands of people lived and worked here, and it held gold, pearls and silk. After the Khmer Empire declined, the jungle swallowed it.

When restorers began work on Angkor, they decided to leave Ta Prohm mostly as they found it, to show how the forest and the temple had grown together. It worked. Today it's one of the most famous ruins in the world, and it appeared in the film Tomb Raider. Engineers now work carefully to keep the trees from tearing it down.

=== 10_houtouwan ===
Number one. Houtouwan, China.

On Shengshan Island, east of Shanghai, a village clings to a steep hillside above the sea. From a distance, it looks like the houses have been painted green. They haven't. They're covered in ivy, from the ground to the rooftops.

Houtouwan was once a busy fishing village, home to around two thousand people. But it was far from schools, shops and roads, and the fishing got harder. In the 1990s, families started moving away, to the main town on the island or to the mainland. By the early 2000s, the village was almost completely empty.

Then the vines came. Year after year, they climbed walls, swallowed doorways and covered entire houses. Photos of the green ghost village spread around the world, and now tourists come to walk its paths. A handful of former residents still live there. Everyone else left the village to the plants.
