=== 00_hook ===
Some treasures were never found. A king's crown jewels swallowed by the tide. A ship carrying the riches of an entire kingdom. Royal jewels stolen from a castle full of guards, just days before the king arrived. And at number one: a whole room made of amber and gold that vanished at the end of the Second World War.

=== 01_king_john ===
Number ten. King John's crown jewels, England.

In October 1216, King John of England was fighting a civil war against his own barons, the same men who had forced him to agree to Magna Carta the year before. He was ill, and he was in a hurry.

To save time, his baggage train took a shortcut across the Wash, a wide tidal estuary on England's east coast, full of mud and quicksand. The tide came in faster than expected. Carts, horses and men were swallowed by the water, and with them, the king's treasure. Chroniclers wrote that he lost the things he held dearest after his own life. Many believe that included the crown jewels.

A few days later, King John was dead. Over eight hundred years, people have searched the fields that were once the Wash. Nothing has ever been found.

=== 02_kruger ===
Number nine. The Kruger Millions, South Africa.

In June 1900, during the Boer War, British troops were closing in on Pretoria, the capital of the Boer republic led by President Paul Kruger. Just before the city fell, gold was taken from the mint and the national bank and loaded onto a train heading east.

What happened to it next is the mystery. Kruger soon left for Europe, and he never came back. Some of the gold was clearly spent on the war. But many believed that a large part of it was buried somewhere in the bush of the Lowveld, to keep it out of British hands.

For more than a century, treasure hunters have dug across the region. Some have even killed each other over maps and rumors. No buried hoard has ever been confirmed. South Africa's own mint says that no accurate account of what happened to the gold has ever been produced.

=== 03_toplitz ===
Number eight. Lake Toplitz, Austria.

Lake Toplitz lies deep in the Austrian Alps, surrounded by steep cliffs and thick forest. It's more than a hundred meters deep, and below about twenty meters, there's almost no oxygen. Fallen trees and anything else that sinks can stay preserved for a very long time.

In the last weeks of the Second World War, witnesses said that German soldiers sank heavy boxes into the lake. In 1959, divers brought some of them up. Inside were millions of forged British pounds, made by Jewish prisoners in a concentration camp, as part of a Nazi plan to wreck Britain's economy.

Rumors say gold and secret documents are still down there. After a diver died in 1963, Austria banned diving in the lake. Later expeditions found rockets, forged notes, and once, a box of beer bottle caps that someone had dumped as a joke.

=== 04_lost_dutchman ===
Number seven. The Lost Dutchman's Gold Mine, USA.

East of Phoenix, Arizona, rise the Superstition Mountains, a maze of desert canyons and cliffs. In the 1870s and 1880s, a German immigrant named Jacob Waltz would come down from these mountains with gold, but he never told anyone where he found it.

When Waltz died in 1891, he supposedly described the mine's location to a friend. She and others searched for weeks, and found nothing. The legend of the Lost Dutchman's Mine was born.

Since then, thousands of people have searched for it. Every year, crowds of hopeful prospectors still head into the mountains. But it's a dangerous place, with extreme heat, little water and sheer drops. Over the years, many people have died in these mountains, some of them while searching for the mine. Many geologists say the area's rocks make a rich gold mine unlikely. The search goes on anyway.

=== 05_irish_crown_jewels ===
Number six. The Irish Crown Jewels, Ireland.

In 1907, Dublin Castle was the heart of British power in Ireland, guarded day and night by police and soldiers. Inside, in a safe in the Office of Arms, lay the Irish Crown Jewels: a star and a badge covered in diamonds, emeralds and rubies, worn by the head of an order of knights.

On July 6, 1907, they were discovered missing. The safe hadn't been forced open. Someone had used a key. And the timing couldn't have been worse: King Edward the Seventh was arriving in Dublin four days later, and the jewels were needed for a ceremony.

The king was furious. Scotland Yard investigated. The man in charge of the jewels lost his job, and rumors of scandal reached high society. But nobody was ever charged, and the jewels have never been seen again.

=== 06_flor_de_la_mar ===
Number five. The Flor de la Mar, Strait of Malacca.

In 1511, the Portuguese commander Afonso de Albuquerque captured Malacca, one of the richest trading ports in Asia. He loaded the treasures of its sultan onto his flagship, the Flor de la Mar, the "Flower of the Sea," to send them home to the king of Portugal.

But the ship was old and leaky. Not long after leaving Malacca, it was caught in a storm in the strait between Malaysia and Sumatra, ran onto shoals, and broke apart. Albuquerque survived. Hundreds of people did not. The treasure went down with the ship.

It's often called one of the richest shipwrecks ever lost, with estimates running into billions of dollars. Treasure hunters have searched for centuries, and some have claimed to be close. But the wreck has never been confirmed, and with several countries likely to claim it, any find would bring a legal battle.

=== 07_gold_train ===
Number four. The Nazi gold train, Poland.

Near the city of Wałbrzych in southwest Poland, the Nazis dug a huge network of tunnels into the mountains during the Second World War, a project called Riese. Thousands of prisoners died building them, and nobody knows for sure what they were for.

Local legend says that in 1945, a train loaded with gold and treasure disappeared into one of these tunnels and was sealed inside. In 2015, two men announced that they had found it using ground-penetrating radar. The story went around the world, and the Polish government said it was very likely real.

In 2016, they dug at the spot for two weeks with bulldozers and drills. They found no train, and no tunnel. Scientists say the train probably never existed. But treasure hunters still search the hills around Wałbrzych.

=== 08_treasure_of_lima ===
Number three. The Treasure of Lima, Cocos Island.

In 1820, revolutionary armies were closing in on Lima, in Peru. To protect the city's riches, the Spanish authorities loaded them onto a British merchant ship, the Mary Dear, commanded by a captain named William Thompson. The cargo reportedly included gold, jewels and two life-size golden statues of the Virgin Mary.

Thompson and his crew couldn't resist. According to the legend, they turned pirate, killed the guards, and sailed for Cocos Island, a remote, rainy island off Costa Rica, where they buried the treasure. Soon after, they were captured. Thompson promised to lead the Spanish to the treasure, then escaped into the jungle.

Since then, hundreds of expeditions have searched Cocos Island. Nothing has been found. Today, the island is a protected national park, and treasure hunting is banned.

=== 09_oak_island ===
Number two. Oak Island, Canada.

In 1795, a teenager named Daniel McGinnis was exploring a small island off Nova Scotia when he found a strange round dip in the ground. He and his friends started digging. A few meters down, they hit a layer of flagstones. Then layers of logs, every few meters, going deeper and deeper.

They had found what became known as the Money Pit. Over the next two centuries, treasure hunters dug again and again. But every time they got deep, sea water flooded the shafts, as if the pit had been designed to protect something.

It has become the longest treasure hunt in history. A future U.S. president, Franklin Roosevelt, invested in one search. Six men have died in accidents on the island. Legend says the treasure won't be found until a seventh dies. More than two hundred years later, nobody has found it.

=== 10_amber_room ===
Number one. The Amber Room, Russia.

In the early 1700s, the King of Prussia gave the Russian tsar an extraordinary gift: a whole room made of amber panels, decorated with gold leaf and mirrors. In the Catherine Palace near Saint Petersburg, it grew to cover about fifty-five square meters, with around six tons of amber. People called it the Eighth Wonder of the World.

In 1941, German troops invaded. They took the Amber Room apart in thirty-six hours, packed it into crates, and shipped it to Königsberg, then a German city. It was put on display in the city's castle.

Then, in 1945, as the Soviet army closed in and the city burned, the Amber Room vanished. Was it destroyed in the fire? Hidden in a tunnel? Lost at sea on a sunken ship? Searches have gone on for eighty years, from Poland to the Czech Republic to the floor of the Baltic Sea. A copy was finished in 2003. The original has never been found.
