=== 00_hook ===
Most ancient places are ruins. You visit, take a photo, and leave. But a few of them never closed. People still eat, sleep, pray, study and drink water in places built hundreds, even thousands of years ago. And every place on this list is older than the one before it. From a restaurant whose oven has been roasting since 1725, to a town where people have lived for seven thousand years, and which gave the world the word Bible. These are the oldest places on Earth that are still in use today.

=== 01_botin ===
Number ten. Sobrino de Botín, Madrid.

Near Madrid's Plaza Mayor runs a narrow street called Calle de Cuchilleros, the street of the knife makers. In 1725, a French cook named Jean Botín and his wife opened an inn here. Guinness World Records lists it as the oldest restaurant in the world that is still open. At first, the inn wasn't even allowed to sell meat or wine. Travelers brought their own food, and the kitchen cooked it for them. The Botíns had no children, so the business passed to a nephew. That's why it's still called Sobrino de Botín: Botín's nephew.

The heart of the place is its wood-fired oven, in use since the day it opened. It still roasts the house dish, suckling pig. Ernest Hemingway liked it so much that he put it in the final chapter of his novel The Sun Also Rises.

=== 02_taos ===
Number nine. Taos Pueblo, New Mexico.

At the foot of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains stand two great buildings made of earth, several stories high. Most of what you see was probably built between the years 1000 and 1450, and the Taos people consider it one of the oldest continuously inhabited communities in the United States.

The walls are adobe: earth mixed with water and straw, often several feet thick, and regularly replastered with fresh mud by hand. Originally, there were no doors at ground level. People climbed ladders and entered through the roof.

About 150 people still live inside the old village full time. By tradition, there is no electricity and no running water within its walls. A stream runs through the middle of the village, flowing down from a sacred lake in the mountains, Blue Lake. In 1906, the US government took the lake for a national forest. The Taos people fought for more than sixty years to get it back, and in 1970, they won.

=== 03_qarawiyyin ===
Number eight. Al-Qarawiyyin, Fez, Morocco.

In the year 859, in the city of Fez, a young woman named Fatima al-Fihri had to decide what to do with her inheritance. Her family had come from Kairouan, in what is now Tunisia, and her father had been a wealthy merchant. She used the money to build a mosque with a school: al-Qarawiyyin.

It grew into one of the great centers of learning in the Islamic world. Guinness World Records calls it the oldest higher-learning institution in the world that has never stopped operating. In 1963, it became part of Morocco's state university system, and students still study here, more than eleven centuries later.

Its library is one of the oldest in the world. When it reopened after a restoration in 2016, the architects had found a hidden room inside, with a carved wooden ceiling from the twelfth century.

=== 04_keiunkan ===
Number seven. Nishiyama Onsen Keiunkan, Japan.

In the mountains of Yamanashi, not far from Mount Fuji, hot water rises out of the ground in a narrow river valley. In the year 705, a man named Fujiwara Mahito opened an inn beside the springs.

More than thirteen centuries later, it's still taking guests. Guinness World Records recognizes Keiunkan as the oldest hotel in the world. It has been run by the same family for 52 generations, sometimes through adopted heirs, as is common in old Japanese family businesses. Tokugawa Ieyasu, the founder of Japan's last shogunate, is said to have stayed here twice.

The building has been rebuilt and modernized, and today it has about 35 rooms. But the main attraction is the same as it was in 705: natural hot spring water, more than a thousand liters a minute, flowing into the baths day and night.

=== 05_catherine ===
Number six. Saint Catherine's Monastery, Egypt.

At the foot of Mount Sinai stands what looks like a fortress in the desert. It's a monastery, built on the orders of the Byzantine emperor Justinian between 548 and 565. It's one of the oldest working Christian monasteries in the world. Monks have lived behind these walls for close to fifteen centuries, and in all that time, it has never been destroyed.

The place is sacred to Christians, Jews and Muslims. Inside grows a bush that tradition links to the burning bush, from which God spoke to Moses. There's even a small mosque within the walls, from the twelfth century.

But the greatest treasure is the library. It's often called the oldest library in the world that has never stopped working. It holds thousands of ancient manuscripts, including surviving pages of the Codex Sinaiticus, one of the oldest known copies of the Bible, written by hand in the fourth century.

=== 06_pantheon ===
Number five. The Pantheon, Rome.

Around the year 125, the Roman emperor Hadrian completed a temple in the heart of Rome. Strangely, the inscription on the front doesn't mention him. It names Marcus Agrippa, who had built an earlier temple on this spot more than a century before.

Step inside, and you're under the dome. It's 43 meters across, and exactly as high as it is wide. Almost two thousand years later, it's still the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. At the top is the oculus, an open hole about nine meters wide. It has never had glass. When it rains, the rain falls straight onto the floor, and small drains carry the water away.

The Pantheon survived because it never stopped being used. In 609, it became a church, and Mass is still celebrated here. The painter Raphael is buried inside, and so are two kings of Italy.

=== 07_dujiangyan ===
Number four. Dujiangyan, China.

Around 256 BC, the Min River was a problem. It rushed down from the mountains, flooded the Chengdu Plain in the wet season, and left it dry the rest of the year. A governor named Li Bing was given the job of taming it.

He didn't build a dam. His workers built a levee in the middle of the river, shaped like the head of a fish. The Fish Mouth splits the river in two. In the dry season, most of the water goes into the inner channel, toward the fields. In a flood, the split flips, and most of it is sent away down the outer river. To carry the water onto the plain, they cut a channel through a mountain. With no explosives, they heated the rock with fire, then cooled it with water until it cracked. It took about eight years.

More than two thousand years later, it still works. Dujiangyan waters about 668,000 hectares of farmland, and it turned the Chengdu Plain into what China calls the Land of Abundance.

=== 08_qanat ===
Number three. The Qanats of Gonabad, Iran.

In the dry northeast of Iran, rain is rare and summers are scorching. Yet around the town of Gonabad, there are green fields. Their water comes from underground, through tunnels dug by hand around two and a half thousand years ago.

This is a qanat. Diggers found water deep under the hills and dug a tunnel to bring it out, sloping so gently that gravity does all the work. No pumps. No power. Along the way, they dug vertical shafts to remove the earth, let air in, and climb down for repairs. From the air, they look like a long line of craters across the desert.

The Qasabeh qanat runs for about 33 kilometers, with 427 wells. The deepest, the mother well, goes down around 300 meters, about the height of the Eiffel Tower. And it still delivers fresh water to the farms of Gonabad. In 2016, it became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

=== 09_arkadiko ===
Number two. The Arkadiko Bridge, Greece.

In the Peloponnese, beside a modern road, stands a small stone bridge you could easily drive past. It's about 22 meters long and 4 meters high, and there's no mortar holding it together, only huge limestone blocks fitted tightly against each other. Later Greeks believed only giants, the Cyclopes, could have moved stones this big.

It was built by the Mycenaeans, the Bronze Age Greeks of Homer's legends, sometime between 1300 and 1190 BC. It carried a road between the cities of Tiryns and Epidaurus, and its shape suggests it was made for chariots. It's a corbel arch: each layer of stone sticks out a little further than the one below, until the two sides meet at the top.

The Mycenaean world collapsed more than three thousand years ago. Its palaces burned, and its writing was forgotten. But this bridge is still standing, and locals still use it.

=== 10_byblos ===
Number one. Byblos, Lebanon.

On the coast of Lebanon, north of Beirut, there's a small harbor town most people have never heard of. But you've used its name. People have lived here for about seven thousand years, and Byblos is often named among the oldest continuously inhabited towns on Earth.

By about 3000 BC, it was a rich port. Its ships carried cedar from the mountains of Lebanon to Egypt. In return came papyrus, the paper of the ancient world. So much of it passed through here that the Greeks called the city Byblos, and byblos became their word for papyrus. From it came biblion, a book, and ta biblia, the books. That's where our word Bible comes from.

In 1923, after a landslide, archaeologists found the tomb of a king of Byblos named Ahiram. His stone coffin carries the earliest known text in the fully developed Phoenician alphabet, the ancestor of Greek and Latin letters, including the ones on your screen right now. And the text is a warning. Whoever disturbs this coffin, it says, will see his scepter broken and his throne overturned.

Today, Byblos is still a living town, with fishing boats in its ancient harbor, and homes built on top of seven thousand years of history.

Most of what humans build doesn't last. Empires fall, cities empty, temples turn into ruins. These places survived for a simple reason: people never stopped needing them.
