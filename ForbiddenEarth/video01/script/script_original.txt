=== 00_hook ===
There's a lake that killed 1,746 people in a single night. A town that has been burning underground since 1962. And an island so deadly that no one was allowed to set foot on it for 48 years. You've probably never heard of most of them. Today, we count down ten of the deadliest places on Earth that almost nobody talks about. And number one could still put two million people at risk.

=== 01_gruinard ===
Number ten. Gruinard Island, Scotland.

It looks like a peaceful patch of green off the coast of Scotland. But in 1942, during World War Two, British scientists brought sheep here and exploded bombs filled with anthrax spores next to them. The sheep died within days. The test worked. Too well. The spores soaked into the soil, and the island was sealed off. For 48 years, no one was allowed to land.

At one point, activists left a sealed package of soil from the island outside a military research lab. Tests showed it contained anthrax. In the 1980s, the government finally cleaned Gruinard, soaking it in formaldehyde and seawater. On April 24th, 1990, it was declared safe, and the heirs of the original owners bought it back for just £500. The government even promised to return and do more work if needed, any time within 150 years. Just in case.

=== 02_kawah_ijen ===
Number nine. Kawah Ijen, Indonesia.

At night, this volcano in Indonesia glows with electric blue fire. It isn't lava. It's sulfur gas burning as it meets the air. By day, you can see what sits inside the crater: a turquoise lake about a kilometer wide. It's the largest highly acidic crater lake in the world, with a pH close to zero. One photographer lost a camera and two lenses here, eaten away by the acid.

Right on the shore, miners collect sulfur from pipes fitted over the volcanic vents, often with nothing but a wet cloth over their mouths. In 1976, a sudden eruption from the lake killed 49 sulfur workers. And yet every night, hundreds of tourists hike down into the crater to see the blue fire.

=== 03_zone_rouge ===
Number eight. The Zone Rouge, France.

After World War One, France looked at some of its old battlefields and decided they could not be saved. They became the Zone Rouge, the Red Zone. Originally it covered more than 1,200 square kilometers. Most of that land has since been reclaimed, but around 170 square kilometers is still off-limits to most human activity.

The ground is packed with unexploded shells, and some of them may still contain poison gas. In a few spots, the soil holds so much arsenic that 99 percent of plants die. Every year, farmers along the old front line dig up what they call the iron harvest: tons of shells that never went off. Experts estimate it could take 300 to 700 years to clear them all.

=== 04_plymouth ===
Number seven. Plymouth, Montserrat.

Plymouth was the capital of Montserrat, a small island in the Caribbean. Its volcano, Soufrière Hills, had been quiet for centuries. Then, in July 1995, it woke up. Ash and pyroclastic flows, avalanches of superheated gas and rock, swept down toward the city. By 1997, Plymouth was abandoned for good, much of it buried under volcanic ash.

The entire southern half of the island became an exclusion zone, and the volcano is still active today. But here's the strange part. On paper, Plymouth is still the official capital of Montserrat. That makes it the only ghost town in the world that is still the capital of a territory.

=== 05_centralia ===
Number six. Centralia, Pennsylvania.

In May 1962, the small town of Centralia, Pennsylvania, burned trash in its landfill, an old mining pit. The leading theory is that the fire reached a coal seam underground. It has been burning ever since. The fire crept through the abandoned mines beneath the town, sending smoke and toxic gases up through cracks in the ground. Sinkholes opened in people's yards.

In 1980, about 1,000 people lived here. The government eventually paid most of them to leave, then took over the remaining homes. The town even lost its ZIP code. By 2020, only five residents were left. And the fire? Some estimates say it could keep burning for another 250 years.

=== 06_wittenoom ===
Number five. Wittenoom, Australia.

In the 1950s, Wittenoom was the biggest town in Australia's Pilbara region. It was built around a mine that dug out blue asbestos, the deadliest type of asbestos there is. For years, it was the country's only supplier. The mine closed in 1966, but the fibers stayed, in the dust, in the gorges, and all through the town, carried by wind and water.

More than 2,000 former workers and residents have died of asbestos-related diseases. In 2007, the government did something almost unheard of. It officially erased the town. Wittenoom was removed from maps and road signs. Today, warning signs surround a contaminated area of almost 47,000 hectares.

=== 07_bikini ===
Number four. Bikini Atoll, Marshall Islands.

Bikini Atoll looks like paradise. In 1946, its residents were asked to leave for a while, so the United States could test nuclear weapons. They were told it was important for all of humanity. They never truly came home.

Between 1946 and 1958, 23 nuclear devices were detonated here. The biggest, Castle Bravo in 1954, was about 1,000 times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb, and it left a crater about 1.5 kilometers wide. In the late 1960s, some families were allowed back. About a decade later, tests found dangerous levels of radioactive cesium in their bodies, and they had to leave again. In 2016, radiation in places measured more than six times the safety limit. Yet underwater, coral and fish are thriving. A paradise for everything except people.

=== 08_karachay ===
Number three. Lake Karachay, Russia.

In Russia's southern Ural Mountains, next to the once-secret Mayak nuclear plant, lies what was called the most polluted spot on Earth. Starting in 1951, Mayak dumped radioactive waste straight into this small lake. In 1990, radiation near the discharge point reached about 600 roentgens per hour. Standing there for less than an hour could give you a lethal dose. Guinness World Records lists it as the most radioactive lake ever.

To stop the poisoned sediment from spreading, engineers dropped nearly 10,000 hollow concrete blocks into the water between 1978 and 1986, then buried the rest under rock and soil. By the mid-2010s, Lake Karachay was filled in completely. The lake is gone. The waste underneath it is not.

=== 09_nyos ===
Number two. Lake Nyos, Cameroon.

On the night of August 21st, 1986, a quiet crater lake in Cameroon released a huge cloud of carbon dioxide, between 100,000 and 300,000 tons of it. The gas is heavier than air, so it didn't rise. It rolled down the valleys like an invisible river, about 50 meters thick, at up to 50 kilometers per hour. People in its path lost consciousness almost instantly. Many died in their sleep.

By morning, 1,746 people and 3,500 cattle were dead. In the village of Nyos itself, only six of around 800 residents survived. For centuries, magma had been feeding gas into the lake's still bottom water, trapped there like carbon dioxide in a sealed bottle of soda. Then something disturbed it, and the whole lake turned over. Today, pipes running to the bottom let the gas out slowly, so it can never build up like that again.

=== 10_kivu ===
Number one. Lake Kivu, Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Now imagine a lake like Nyos, but about 2,000 times bigger. Lake Kivu sits on the border of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Deep below its surface, it holds roughly 300 cubic kilometers of carbon dioxide and 60 cubic kilometers of methane. And around two million people live on its shores.

In 2021, the nearby Nyiragongo volcano erupted, and lava pushed underground beneath the lake, waking old fears. A power plant now pumps methane from the deep water to make electricity, which may slowly ease the pressure. Scientists still disagree about the danger. Some call Kivu a ticking time bomb. A 2020 study found the risk does not seem to be growing. Either way, it is one of only three known lakes on Earth loaded with gas like this, and by far the biggest.
