=== 00_hook ===
There's a lake that killed one thousand seven hundred and forty-six people in a single night. A town that has been burning underground since nineteen sixty-two. And an island so deadly that no one was allowed to set foot on it for forty-eight years. You've probably never heard of most of them. Today, we count down ten of the deadliest places on Earth that almost nobody talks about. And number one could still put two million people at risk.

=== 01_gruinard ===
Number ten. [Gruinard](/ɡɹˈɪnjəɹd/) Island, Scotland.

It looks like a peaceful patch of green off the coast of Scotland. But in nineteen forty-two, during World War Two, British scientists brought sheep here and exploded bombs filled with anthrax spores next to them. The sheep died within days. The test worked. Too well. The spores soaked into the soil, and the island was sealed off. For forty-eight years, no one was allowed to land.

At one point, activists left a sealed package of soil from the island outside a military research lab. Tests showed it contained anthrax. In the nineteen-eighties, the government finally cleaned [Gruinard](/ɡɹˈɪnjəɹd/), soaking it in formaldehyde and seawater. On April twenty-fourth, nineteen ninety, it was declared safe, and the heirs of the original owners bought it back for just five hundred pounds. The government even promised to return and do more work if needed, any time within one hundred and fifty years. Just in case.

=== 02_kawah_ijen ===
Number nine. [Kawah](/kˈɑwɑ/) [Ijen](/ˈiʤɛn/), Indonesia.

At night, this volcano in Indonesia glows with electric blue fire. It isn't lava. It's sulfur gas burning as it meets the air. By day, you can see what sits inside the crater: a turquoise lake about a kilometer wide. It's the largest highly acidic crater lake in the world, with a p H close to zero. One photographer lost a camera and two lenses here, eaten away by the acid.

Right on the shore, miners collect sulfur from pipes fitted over the volcanic vents, often with nothing but a wet cloth over their mouths. In nineteen seventy-six, a sudden eruption from the lake killed forty-nine sulfur workers. And yet every night, hundreds of tourists hike down into the crater to see the blue fire.

=== 03_zone_rouge ===
Number eight. The Zone Rouge, France.

After World War One, France looked at some of its old battlefields and decided they could not be saved. They became the Zone Rouge, the Red Zone. Originally it covered more than one thousand two hundred square kilometers. Most of that land has since been reclaimed, but around one hundred and seventy square kilometers is still off-limits to most human activity.

The ground is packed with unexploded shells, and some of them may still contain poison gas. In a few spots, the soil holds so much arsenic that ninety-nine percent of plants die. Every year, farmers along the old front line dig up what they call the iron harvest: tons of shells that never went off. Experts estimate it could take three hundred to seven hundred years to clear them all.

=== 04_plymouth ===
Number seven. Plymouth, [Montserrat](/mˌɑntsəɹˈæt/).

Plymouth was the capital of [Montserrat](/mˌɑntsəɹˈæt/), a small island in the Caribbean. Its volcano, [Soufrière](/sˌufɹiˈɛɹ/) Hills, had been quiet for centuries. Then, in July nineteen ninety-five, it woke up. Ash and pyroclastic flows, avalanches of superheated gas and rock, swept down toward the city. By nineteen ninety-seven, Plymouth was abandoned for good, much of it buried under volcanic ash.

The entire southern half of the island became an exclusion zone, and the volcano is still active today. But here's the strange part. On paper, Plymouth is still the official capital of [Montserrat](/mˌɑntsəɹˈæt/). That makes it the only ghost town in the world that is still the capital of a territory.

=== 05_centralia ===
Number six. [Centralia](/sɛntɹˈAliə/), Pennsylvania.

In May nineteen sixty-two, the small town of [Centralia](/sɛntɹˈAliə/), Pennsylvania, burned trash in its landfill, an old mining pit. The leading theory is that the fire reached a coal seam underground. It has been burning ever since. The fire crept through the abandoned mines beneath the town, sending smoke and toxic gases up through cracks in the ground. Sinkholes opened in people's yards.

In nineteen eighty, about one thousand people lived here. The government eventually paid most of them to leave, then took over the remaining homes. The town even lost its ZIP code. By twenty twenty, only five residents were left. And the fire? Some estimates say it could keep burning for another two hundred and fifty years.

=== 06_wittenoom ===
Number five. [Wittenoom](/wˈɪtᵊnˌum/), Australia.

In the nineteen-fifties, [Wittenoom](/wˈɪtᵊnˌum/) was the biggest town in Australia's [Pilbara](/pˈɪlbəɹə/) region. It was built around a mine that dug out blue asbestos, the deadliest type of asbestos there is. For years, it was the country's only supplier. The mine closed in nineteen sixty-six, but the fibers stayed, in the dust, in the gorges, and all through the town, carried by wind and water.

More than two thousand former workers and residents have died of asbestos-related diseases. In two thousand and seven, the government did something almost unheard of. It officially erased the town. [Wittenoom](/wˈɪtᵊnˌum/) was removed from maps and road signs. Today, warning signs surround a contaminated area of almost forty-seven thousand hectares.

=== 07_bikini ===
Number four. Bikini Atoll, Marshall Islands.

Bikini Atoll looks like paradise. In nineteen forty-six, its residents were asked to leave for a while, so the United States could test nuclear weapons. They were told it was important for all of humanity. They never truly came home.

Between nineteen forty-six and nineteen fifty-eight, twenty-three nuclear devices were detonated here. The biggest, Castle Bravo in nineteen fifty-four, was about one thousand times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb, and it left a crater about one point five kilometers wide. In the late nineteen-sixties, some families were allowed back. About a decade later, tests found dangerous levels of radioactive cesium in their bodies, and they had to leave again. In twenty sixteen, radiation in places measured more than six times the safety limit. Yet underwater, coral and fish are thriving. A paradise for everything except people.

=== 08_karachay ===
Number three. Lake [Karachay](/kˌɑɹəʧˈI/), Russia.

In Russia's southern [Ural](/jˈʊɹəl/) Mountains, next to the once-secret [Mayak](/mɑjˈɑk/) nuclear plant, lies what was called the most polluted spot on Earth. Starting in nineteen fifty-one, [Mayak](/mɑjˈɑk/) dumped radioactive waste straight into this small lake. In nineteen ninety, radiation near the discharge point reached about six hundred roentgens per hour. Standing there for less than an hour could give you a lethal dose. [Guinness](/ɡˈɪnɪs/) World Records lists it as the most radioactive lake ever.

To stop the poisoned sediment from spreading, engineers dropped nearly ten thousand hollow concrete blocks into the water between nineteen seventy-eight and nineteen eighty-six, then buried the rest under rock and soil. By the mid twenty-tens, Lake [Karachay](/kˌɑɹəʧˈI/) was filled in completely. The lake is gone. The waste underneath it is not.

=== 09_nyos ===
Number two. Lake [Nyos](/nˈiOs/), Cameroon.

On the night of August twenty-first, nineteen eighty-six, a quiet crater lake in Cameroon released a huge cloud of carbon dioxide, between one hundred thousand and three hundred thousand tons of it. The gas is heavier than air, so it didn't rise. It rolled down the valleys like an invisible river, about fifty meters thick, at up to fifty kilometers per hour. People in its path lost consciousness almost instantly. Many died in their sleep.

By morning, one thousand seven hundred and forty-six people and three thousand five hundred cattle were dead. In the village of [Nyos](/nˈiOs/) itself, only six of around eight hundred residents survived. For centuries, magma had been feeding gas into the lake's still bottom water, trapped there like carbon dioxide in a sealed bottle of soda. Then something disturbed it, and the whole lake turned over. Today, pipes running to the bottom let the gas out slowly, so it can never build up like that again.

=== 10_kivu ===
Number one. Lake [Kivu](/kˈivu/), Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Now imagine a lake like [Nyos](/nˈiOs/), but about two thousand times bigger. Lake [Kivu](/kˈivu/) sits on the border of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Deep below its surface, it holds roughly three hundred cubic kilometers of carbon dioxide and sixty cubic kilometers of methane. And around two million people live on its shores.

In twenty twenty-one, the nearby [Nyiragongo](/nˌɪɹəɡˈɑŋɡO/) volcano erupted, and lava pushed underground beneath the lake, waking old fears. A power plant now pumps methane from the deep water to make electricity, which may slowly ease the pressure. Scientists still disagree about the danger. Some call [Kivu](/kˈivu/) a ticking time bomb. A twenty twenty study found the risk does not seem to be growing. Either way, it is one of only three known lakes on Earth loaded with gas like this, and by far the biggest.
