=== 00_hook ===
We have climbed Everest. We have walked on the Moon. And yet, here on Earth, there are places where no human has ever stood. A mountain whose summit a king said must never be touched. A hole in the sea so deep that nothing we've sent down has found the bottom. A mountain range the size of the Alps that no one has ever seen. And at number one: a place so big it covers most of our planet.

=== 01_hranice ===
Number ten. The [Hranice](/hɹˈɑnjɪtsə/) Abyss, Czech Republic.

In a quiet forest in the east of the Czech Republic, there's a crack in the limestone. Climb down about seventy meters, and you reach a small green lake. That lake is the door to the deepest flooded cave on Earth.

Divers have tried to follow it down. The deepest of them, the Polish explorer [Krzysztof](/kʃˈɪʃtɔf/) [Starnawski](/stɑɹnˈɑvski/), went down more than two hundred meters, and needed hours of slow decompression just to get back to the surface alive.

So the explorers started sending robots. In twenty sixteen, a robot reached four hundred meters, and the [Hranice](/hɹˈɑnjɪtsə/) Abyss became the deepest known underwater cave in the world. In twenty twenty-two, another robot went down four hundred and fifty meters below the surface of the lake. It still didn't touch the bottom.

Some scientists think the abyss could go down a kilometer, or even more. Nobody knows. No human, and no machine, has ever reached the end.

=== 02_machapuchare ===
Number nine. [Machapuchare](/mˌɑʧəpuʧˈɑɹA/), Nepal.

Above the lakeside town of [Pokhara](/pˈOkəɹə/) rises a mountain with a split summit that looks like the tail of a fish. [Machapuchare](/mˌɑʧəpuʧˈɑɹA/) is almost seven thousand meters high. Local people believe it is a home of the god Shiva.

In nineteen fifty-seven, a British team was allowed to try to climb it, on one condition from the King of Nepal: they could climb the mountain, but they must not step on the summit.

Two climbers, Wilfrid Noyce and David Cox, fought their way up the north ridge until they were about fifty meters from the top. Then they turned around. They had given their word.

After that, Nepal closed the mountain. No climbing permits have been issued for it since. Thousands of trekkers stare up at [Machapuchare](/mˌɑʧəpuʧˈɑɹA/) every year, but officially, no human has ever stood on its summit.

=== 03_taam_ja ===
Number eight. The Taam [Ja](/hˈɑ/) Blue Hole, Mexico.

In a shallow bay on the Caribbean coast of Mexico, close to the border with Belize, there's a dark circle in the water. Its Mayan name, Taam [Ja](/hˈɑ/), means deep water. That turned out to be an understatement.

When scientists first measured it in twenty twenty-one, their sonar stopped working at around two hundred and seventy-five meters. The hole simply swallowed the echoes.

So in December twenty twenty-three, they lowered an instrument on a cable. All five hundred meters of cable ran out. The instrument hung about four hundred and twenty meters below the surface, and it still hadn't touched the bottom.

That makes Taam [Ja](/hˈɑ/) the deepest known blue hole on Earth. Scientists think it may connect to a hidden maze of underwater caves. Whatever is down there, no one has seen it.

=== 04_thwaites ===
Number seven. Beneath the Thwaites Glacier, Antarctica.

In West Antarctica there's a glacier about the size of Florida. People call it the Doomsday Glacier, because if all of its ice melted, global sea levels would rise by more than half a meter.

What happens to Thwaites depends on a place no one can visit: the hidden line deep under the ice, where the glacier stops resting on rock and starts floating on the sea.

In twenty twenty, scientists drilled a hole almost six hundred meters straight down through the ice and lowered a thin robot called Icefin into the darkness. It swam to that line and sent back the first pictures ever taken there: warm water eating away at the ice, carving it into giant steps, like a staircase, and splitting it with deep cracks.

No human could ever go there. Only the robot has seen it.

=== 05_kawagarbo ===
Number six. [Kawagarbo](/kˌɑwəɡˈɑɹbO/), China.

In the far northwest of Yunnan province, close to Tibet, stands a white mountain called [Kawagarbo](/kˌɑwəɡˈɑɹbO/). It's six thousand seven hundred and forty meters high, and for Tibetan Buddhists, it is one of the most sacred mountains on Earth, the home of a warrior god.

In the late nineteen eighties, climbers from Japan and China came to try to reach the top. Many local people protested.

In January nineteen ninety-one, during their second attempt, an avalanche swept over their camp at night. All seventeen members of the expedition were lost. Local people saw it as a sign of the mountain's anger.

In two thousand and one, the local government banned climbing on [Kawagarbo](/kˌɑwəɡˈɑɹbO/) for good. Its summit has never been reached, and it's likely to stay that way.

=== 06_denman ===
Number five. Denman Canyon, Antarctica.

The lowest land you can stand on is the shore of the Dead Sea, more than four hundred meters below sea level. But it's not the lowest land on Earth.

In twenty nineteen, scientists published a new map of the ground hidden beneath the Antarctic ice. Under the Denman Glacier in East Antarctica, they found a canyon that plunges about three thousand five hundred meters below sea level. That's about eight times lower than the Dead Sea.

It's the deepest point on land anywhere on Earth. But you could never stand on it. The whole canyon is filled with the moving ice of the glacier. Scientists only know its shape from radar, satellites, and the way the ice above it moves.

=== 07_zacaton ===
Number four. El [Zacatón](/zˌɑkətˈOn/), Mexico.

On a ranch in northeastern Mexico, there's a perfectly round pool, warm, and smelling faintly of sulfur. It looks peaceful. It's actually the top of one of the deepest water-filled sinkholes in the world.

For years, divers tried to reach its bottom. In nineteen ninety-four, two of the world's best cave divers went down together. One of them, Jim Bowden, reached two hundred and eighty-two meters, a world record at the time, and still didn't see the bottom. His partner, Sheck [Exley](/ˈɛksli/), never came back.

In two thousand and seven, a NASA-funded robot went down instead. It had been built to practice for exploring the ice-covered ocean of Jupiter's moon Europa. The robot found the bottom of El [Zacatón](/zˌɑkətˈOn/) about three hundred and eighteen meters down.

A machine has touched it. A human never has.

=== 08_gamburtsev ===
Number three. The [Gamburtsev](/ɡæmbˈʊɹtsɛv/) Mountains, Antarctica.

In nineteen fifty-eight, a Soviet expedition was crossing the middle of East Antarctica, setting off small explosions and listening to the echoes to measure how thick the ice was. The echoes came back wrong. Under the ice, there were mountains.

Decades later, aircraft with ice-penetrating radar flew over the area again and again. They revealed a mountain range more than a thousand kilometers long, with peaks almost three kilometers high, sharp ridges, and deep valleys. It's about the size of the Alps.

But the whole range is buried under the highest part of the Antarctic ice sheet. Even the tallest peaks lie hundreds of meters under the ice, and the valleys lie under kilometers of it. No human eye has ever seen these mountains. We only know they're there because of echoes.

=== 09_gangkhar ===
Number two. Gangkhar [Puensum](/pˈunsəm/), Bhutan.

On the border between Bhutan and Tibet stands the highest unclimbed mountain on Earth. Gangkhar [Puensum](/pˈunsəm/) is seven thousand five hundred and seventy meters high. Its name means the White Peak of the Three Spiritual Brothers.

When Bhutan first opened its mountains to climbers, teams came to try. In the mid-nineteen eighties, four expeditions attempted it. Storms, confusing maps, and the mountain itself turned every one of them back.

Then, out of respect for local spiritual beliefs, Bhutan closed its highest peaks. In nineteen ninety-four, it banned climbing above six thousand meters. In two thousand and three, it banned mountaineering completely.

Everest has been climbed thousands of times. The top of Gangkhar [Puensum](/pˈunsəm/) has never felt a single human footstep.

=== 10_deep_seafloor ===
Number one. The deep seafloor.

Here's the biggest place no human has ever set foot on. It's not a mountain or a hole. It's most of our planet.

Below about two hundred meters, sunlight fades and the deep sea begins. Its floor covers roughly two thirds of the Earth's surface, more than all the continents put together.

In twenty twenty-five, scientists added up every deep-sea dive they could find records of, more than forty thousand since nineteen fifty-eight, with submarines, robots, and cameras. Their result was shocking. In all that time, humans have seen less than one thousandth of one percent of the deep seafloor. All together, that's an area about the size of the small US state of Rhode Island.

Everything else, mountains, canyons, vents, and creatures we don't even have names for, is still waiting in the dark. No one has ever been there.
