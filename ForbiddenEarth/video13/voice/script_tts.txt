=== 00_hook ===
Some roads take you somewhere. These ten roads might not let you come back. A highway built across frozen lakes that melts away every spring. A tunnel that villagers carved through a cliff with hammers and chisels. A road in Siberia that people call the Road of Bones. And at number one: a narrow strip of dirt in the Andes so deadly that a major bank named it the most dangerous road in the world.

=== 01_caracoles ===
Number ten. Los [Caracoles](/kˌɑɹəkˈOlɛs/), Chile.

High in the Andes, on the main road between Santiago in Chile and Mendoza in Argentina, the highway suddenly folds back and forth on itself. Twenty-nine hairpin bends, stacked one above the other on a steep mountainside. From above, it looks like the shell of a snail, and that's exactly what the name means: the Snails.

Trucks, buses and cars crawl up these turns to a border crossing about three thousand two hundred meters above sea level. The road itself is well kept. The danger is the weather. Up here, storms can arrive in minutes, and snow and falling rocks can close the pass for days.

In September twenty thirteen, a sudden snowstorm shut the border, and nearly fifteen thousand travelers from Chile were left stranded on the Argentine side.

=== 02_ice_road ===
Number nine. The [Tibbitt](/tˈɪbɪt/) to [Contwoyto](/kɑntwˈYtO/) Winter Road, Canada.

In the far north of Canada, there's a road that only exists for about two months a year. Every January, crews build it across the frozen wilderness north of Yellowknife. About eighty-five percent of it runs over frozen lakes.

It's said to be the longest heavy-haul ice road in the world, between four and six hundred kilometers long. For eight to ten weeks, trucks carry fuel and supplies to the diamond mines of the Arctic. Then spring comes, the ice melts, and the road disappears into the water.

The rules are strict. Fully loaded trucks may not go faster than twenty-five kilometers an hour, and they keep about five hundred meters apart, so the ice is never overloaded. Under the wheels, there's nothing but ice, and below it, black, freezing water.

=== 03_sani_pass ===
Number eight. [Sani](/sˈɑni/) Pass, South Africa and [Lesotho](/ləsˈutu/).

In the [Drakensberg](/dɹˈɑkənzbˌɜɹɡ/) mountains, a rough gravel track climbs from South Africa toward the mountain kingdom of [Lesotho](/ləsˈutu/). In about nine kilometers, it rises more than thirteen hundred meters, through tight hairpin bends cut into the cliffs.

[Sani](/sˈɑni/) Pass starts at around fifteen hundred meters and ends at two thousand eight hundred and seventy-six, at the [Lesotho](/ləsˈutu/) border post. South Africa generally allows only four-by-four vehicles on it. Rain turns the dirt to mud, and winter can cover it in snow and ice.

At the very top, there's a reward for anyone who makes it: a small mountain lodge that calls itself the highest pub in Africa.

=== 04_dalton ===
Number seven. The Dalton Highway, USA.

In northern Alaska, a single road runs four hundred and fourteen miles, about six hundred and sixty-six kilometers, from the forests near Fairbanks all the way to the oil fields of [Prudhoe](/pɹˈʌdO/) Bay on the Arctic coast.

It was built in nineteen seventy-four, in just five months, as a haul road for the Trans-Alaska Pipeline. Most of it is still gravel. It climbs over [Atigun](/ˈætɪɡən/) Pass in the Brooks Range, more than fourteen hundred meters high, and crosses the Arctic tundra.

What makes it dangerous is the emptiness. After the truck stop at [Coldfoot](/kˈOldfˌʊt/), there are no services for about two hundred and forty miles, almost four hundred kilometers. No gas, no repair shop, often no phone signal. In winter, temperatures can fall far below minus forty. If you break down out here, you're on your own.

=== 05_skippers ===
Number six. Skippers Canyon Road, New Zealand.

Near [Queenstown](/kwˈinztˌWn/), a narrow dirt road clings to the wall of a canyon high above the [Shotover](/ʃˈɑtˌOvəɹ/) River, once called the richest river in the world because of its gold.

It was built between eighteen eighty-three and eighteen ninety to reach the gold diggings, much of it by Chinese workers, and cut straight into the rock. It was barely wide enough for one vehicle then, and it hasn't grown much since. On one side, a wall of rock. On the other, a drop of hundreds of meters.

It's one of the few roads in New Zealand where rental car companies won't let you drive their cars. If you want to see Skippers Canyon, most people let a local guide do the driving.

=== 06_zoji_la ===
Number five. [Zoji La](/zˈOʤi lˈɑ/), India.

In the Himalayas, a pass three thousand five hundred and twenty-eight meters high connects the valley of [Kashmir](/kæʃmˈɪɹ/) with Ladakh. For the people of Ladakh, it's a lifeline. It's also one of the most frightening drives in Asia.

The road is narrow and unpaved in places, with sheer drops and no barriers, and it's shared by army convoys, trucks and buses. Every winter, heavy snow buries the pass, sometimes deeper than a truck is tall, and the road is closed for months. Avalanches are a constant threat.

That may finally change. In June twenty twenty-six, engineers broke through a tunnel more than thirteen kilometers long deep beneath the pass. When it opens, a crossing that can take hours will take about fifteen minutes.

=== 07_guoliang ===
Number four. The [Guoliang](/ɡwˌOliˈɑŋ/) Tunnel, China.

For centuries, the village of [Guoliang](/ɡwˌOliˈɑŋ/) sat on top of a cliff in the [Taihang](/tˌIhˈɑŋ/) Mountains, cut off from the world. The only way down was a steep stairway carved into the rock.

In nineteen seventy-two, the villagers decided to build a road themselves. They sold their livestock to buy hammers and chisels. Thirteen men started the work, hanging on ropes from the cliff face, cutting into the rock by hand. At one point, they advanced just one meter every three days.

Five years later, on May first, nineteen seventy-seven, the tunnel opened: about one point two kilometers long, five meters high, and four meters wide, with more than thirty openings like windows looking out over the drop. One of the builders died during the work. Today, tour buses squeeze through the tunnel that the villagers carved with their own hands.

=== 08_kolyma ===
Number three. The [Kolyma](/kˌOlɪmˈɑ/) Highway, Russia.

In the far east of Siberia, a road runs about two thousand kilometers from the port of [Magadan](/mˌɑɡədˈɑn/) toward [Yakutsk](/jəkˈutsk/), one of the coldest cities on Earth. Locals call it the Road of Bones.

It was built from nineteen thirty-two to nineteen fifty-three by prisoners of the Soviet gulag, forced to work in brutal cold. Many of them died, and some were buried along the road they were building.

Today, the [Kolyma](/kˌOlɪmˈɑ/) Highway is mostly gravel. In winter, it's covered with ice and the temperature can drop below minus fifty. In summer, parts of it turn to mud. Abandoned villages and ruined camps line the way. For the few drivers who still make the journey, it can take four or five days.

=== 09_fairy_meadows ===
Number two. Fairy Meadows Road, Pakistan.

In northern Pakistan, near the bottom of [Nanga Parbat](/nˈʌŋɡə pˈʌɹbət/), the ninth highest mountain on Earth, a gravel track leaves the Karakoram Highway at [Raikot](/ɹˈIkɑt/) Bridge and climbs toward the village of [Tato](/tˈɑtO/).

It's about sixteen kilometers long. It was built by local villagers, it's only as wide as a jeep, and it has no guardrails at all. On one side, the mountain. On the other, a drop straight down into the valley.

Visitors aren't allowed to drive it themselves. Local drivers carry them up in old jeeps, around corners where the wheels pass inches from the edge. And at the end of the road, the adventure isn't over. From [Tato](/tˈɑtO/), it's still a hike of several hours to the meadows below [Nanga Parbat](/nˈʌŋɡə pˈʌɹbət/).

=== 10_yungas ===
Number one. The [Yungas](/jˈuŋɡɑs/) Road, Bolivia.

In the nineteen thirties, during the [Chaco](/ʧˈɑkO/) War, Paraguayan prisoners were forced to cut a road down the side of the Andes, from the high plateau near La Paz to the jungle region of the [Yungas](/jˈuŋɡɑs/).

The road drops about three thousand five hundred meters. In places, it's less than three meters wide, with a cliff on one side and a fall of hundreds of meters on the other. For decades, it was the main route north, used by trucks and crowded buses. Fog, rain, mud and waterfalls made it worse. By some estimates, two to three hundred people died on it every year.

In nineteen ninety-five, the Inter-American Development Bank called it the most dangerous road in the world. People simply call it Death Road. In the two thousands, a new road was finished nearby, and most traffic moved there. But the old road is still open, and every year thousands of tourists ride down it on mountain bikes, past the crosses on the edge.
