=== 00_hook ===
Some of the most beautiful places on Earth are off-limits. Some are guarded by a lottery. Some by law. Some by the gods. One by thousands of venomous snakes. And at number one, a place so precious that no one alive has ever seen inside it. These are ten beautiful places you're not allowed to visit.

=== 01_the_wave ===
Number ten. The Wave, Arizona.

On the border of Arizona and Utah, the rock looks like it's moving. Layers of red, orange and cream sandstone curve and ripple like frozen water. It's called The Wave. But you can't just walk in.

The sandstone ridges are thin and fragile, so the U S Bureau of Land Management strictly limits visits. Today, only sixty-four people a day may enter the area around The Wave, and every permit is handed out by lottery. Most are drawn online, four months in advance. The rest go to a daily draw for people already nearby. In a single year, more than two hundred thousand people have entered the lottery, back when only twenty permits a day were available. So yes, you can see The Wave. You just have to be very lucky.

=== 02_fly_geyser ===
Number nine. Fly Geyser, Nevada.

It looks like something from another planet, but it was made by accident. In nineteen sixty-four, a well was drilled here, in the desert of northern Nevada, and it hit scalding geothermal water. The water has been flowing ever since. Over the decades, minerals in it built up into strange, multicolored cones, streaked red, orange and green, that are still growing today.

The catch is that Fly Geyser sits on private land, a ranch called Fly Ranch. For nearly twenty years, it was closed to the public. In twenty sixteen, the ranch was bought by the Burning Man Project, the organization behind the famous festival held in the nearby desert. Today, the only way to see the geyser up close is on a small guided walk. And some of its pools are close to boiling.

=== 03_niihau ===
Number eight. Niihau, Hawaii.

Just seventeen miles off the coast of [Kauai](/kɑwˈɑi/) lies a Hawaiian island that most people will never set foot on. In eighteen sixty-four, Elizabeth [Sinclair](/sˈɪnklɛɹ/) bought Niihau from King [Kamehameha](/kəmˌAhəmˈAhə/) the Fifth for ten thousand dollars in gold. Her descendants, the [Robinson](/ɹˈɑbɪnsən/) family, still own it today, and they decide who comes ashore.

About seventy people live on Niihau, and they speak Hawaiian as their first language, every day. The island earned its nickname, the Forbidden Isle, because for generations outsiders were kept away, to protect the people living there from disease and outside influence. Today, a few tightly controlled helicopter tours, hunting trips and [dives](/dˈIvz/) are allowed. Everything else is up to the family that has owned the island for more than one hundred and sixty years.

=== 04_surtsey ===
Number seven. Surtsey, Iceland.

In November nineteen sixty-three, an eruption broke the surface of the Atlantic Ocean off the south coast of Iceland. A day later, a ridge of new rock stood forty meters high. By the time the eruptions ended, Earth had a brand-new island. Iceland named it Surtsey, after [Surtr](/sˈɜɹtəɹ/), a fire giant from Norse mythology.

Scientists realized they had been handed something priceless: a chance to watch life arrive on bare, sterile land, from the very beginning. So in nineteen sixty-five, Iceland put the island under protection, and it has been off-limits ever since to everyone except approved researchers. Even they must be careful not to bring a single seed. Moulds and bacteria came first. The first plant appeared in nineteen sixty-five. Birds carried in more seeds. Today, seals breed on Surtsey's beaches, and nearly ninety species of birds have been recorded there. But the sea is slowly wearing the island away.

=== 05_lascaux ===
Number six. Lascaux Cave, France.

In nineteen forty, a group of teenagers in southwestern France followed their dog into a hole in the ground. Inside, they found walls covered with paintings of horses, bulls and deer, around seventeen thousand years old. It was one of the greatest discoveries of prehistoric art ever made.

Lascaux opened to the public in nineteen forty-eight, and the crowds came. By the early nineteen-sixties, more than one thousand five hundred people a day were squeezing into the cave. The heat, the humidity and the microbes they brought with them began to harm the paintings, and green mould spread across the walls. In nineteen sixty-three, Lascaux was closed to the public, and it has stayed closed ever since. Only researchers and conservators may go inside, in sterile overalls and gloves. Everyone else visits a full-size replica nearby.

=== 06_ise ===
Number five. Ise Grand Shrine, Japan.

Deep in a forest in central Japan stands the most sacred shrine of the Shinto religion. Its inner shrine, called [Naiku](/nˈIku/), is dedicated to [Amaterasu](/ˌɑmɑtɛɹˈɑsu/), the sun goddess. Millions of pilgrims come here every year. But the heart of the shrine is off-limits. Ordinary visitors stop at the outer fences. Beyond them, only priests and the imperial family may go.

And here's what makes Ise unlike anywhere else on Earth: every twenty years, the shrine is rebuilt from scratch. All one hundred and twenty-five buildings of the complex are recreated from fresh cypress wood, exactly as before, on a plot right next to the old one. The tradition is about one thousand three hundred years old, with the first recorded rebuilding in the year six hundred and ninety. The current rebuilding is already underway, and in twenty thirty-three the goddess will be moved into her new home. Then the cycle begins again.

=== 07_snake_island ===
Number four. [Ilha](/ˈiljə/) [da](/dɑ/) [Queimada](/kAmˈɑdə/) [Grande](/ɡɹˈɑnʤi/), Brazil.

About thirty-five kilometers off the coast of [São](/sˈW/) [Paulo](/pˈWlO/) state sits a lush green island with rocky shores and clear blue water. It looks like paradise. Brazilians call it Snake Island.

It is the only home of the golden [lancehead](/lˈænshˌɛd/), a pit viper found nowhere else on Earth. Cut off from the mainland for around eleven thousand years, the snakes learned to hunt the birds that stop on the island. Their venom is said to be up to five times more potent than that of their mainland relatives. Scientists estimate there are a few thousand of them, on an island of just forty-three hectares. The golden [lancehead](/lˈænshˌɛd/) is critically endangered, and Brazil has banned visits. Only the navy and a handful of vetted scientists are allowed to land. Everyone else has to admire paradise from a boat.

=== 08_naica ===
Number three. The Cave of the Crystals, Mexico.

In two thousand, two brothers drilling a tunnel three hundred meters underground in the [Naica](/nˈIkə/) mine, in northern Mexico, broke into a hidden chamber. Inside were crystals so big it looked like a set from a fantasy film. Giant beams of gypsum, some around eleven meters long, crisscrossed the cave from wall to wall. One geologist called it the Sistine Chapel of crystals.

They grew slowly, over hundreds of thousands of years, in hot, mineral-rich water that once filled the cave. And the cave is still brutally hot: up to fifty-eight degrees Celsius, with humidity above ninety percent. Without protection, it's dangerous to stay longer than about ten minutes, so scientists worked in cooling suits, in short shifts. Then, in twenty fifteen, the mine shut down, the pumps were switched off, and the water returned. Today, one of the most beautiful caves on Earth is flooded again, and no one can go in.

=== 09_kailash ===
Number two. Mount [Kailash](/kIlˈɑʃ/), Tibet.

In the far west of Tibet rises a mountain shaped almost like a pyramid, its faces striped with snow. Mount [Kailash](/kIlˈɑʃ/) is six thousand six hundred and thirty-eight meters tall, more than two kilometers lower than Everest. And yet, while thousands of people have stood on top of Everest, nobody has ever climbed [Kailash](/kIlˈɑʃ/).

The reason isn't difficulty. It's faith. [Kailash](/kIlˈɑʃ/) is sacred to four religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and [Bön](/bˈɜn/). Hindus believe it is the home of Shiva. For Buddhists, it is linked to Mount [Meru](/mˈAɹu/), the center of the universe. More than a billion people consider it holy, and climbing it would be seen as sacrilege, so climbing permits are not granted. Instead, pilgrims walk around the mountain, a journey of about three days. Some cover the whole route by bowing down to the ground with every few steps.

=== 10_qin ===
Number one. The Tomb of Qin [Shi](/ʃˈɜɹ/) [Huang](/hwˈɑŋ/), China.

In nineteen seventy-four, farmers digging a well near the city of [Xi'an](/ʃiˈɑn/) hit fragments of clay. They had found the [Terracotta](/tˌɛɹəkˈɑtə/) Army: an underground force of at least eight thousand figures, made to guard the first emperor of China, Qin [Shi](/ʃˈɜɹ/) [Huang](/hwˈɑŋ/), after his death in two hundred and ten B C.

But the soldiers are only the guards. The emperor himself lies under a huge mound nearby, in a burial chamber that has never been opened. More than two thousand years ago, the historian [Sima](/səmˈɑ/) [Qian](/ʧjˈɛn/) wrote that seven hundred thousand workers built the tomb, and that mercury was used to create its hundred rivers, including the Yellow River and the [Yangtze](/jˈæŋsi/). It sounded like legend. But when scientists tested the soil above the chamber, they found far more mercury than in the ground around it. China has no plans to open the tomb. Any breach could let in air and water and destroy what's inside. So the greatest tomb in China stays sealed, and no one alive has ever seen it.
