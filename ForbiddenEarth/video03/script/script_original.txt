=== 00_hook ===
Some of the most beautiful places on Earth are off-limits. Some are guarded by a lottery. Some by law. Some by the gods. One by thousands of venomous snakes. And at number one, a place so precious that no one alive has ever seen inside it. These are ten beautiful places you're not allowed to visit.

=== 01_the_wave ===
Number ten. The Wave, Arizona.

On the border of Arizona and Utah, the rock looks like it's moving. Layers of red, orange and cream sandstone curve and ripple like frozen water. It's called The Wave. But you can't just walk in.

The sandstone ridges are thin and fragile, so the US Bureau of Land Management strictly limits visits. Today, only 64 people a day may enter the area around The Wave, and every permit is handed out by lottery. Most are drawn online, four months in advance. The rest go to a daily draw for people already nearby. In a single year, more than 200,000 people have entered the lottery, back when only 20 permits a day were available. So yes, you can see The Wave. You just have to be very lucky.

=== 02_fly_geyser ===
Number nine. Fly Geyser, Nevada.

It looks like something from another planet, but it was made by accident. In 1964, a well was drilled here, in the desert of northern Nevada, and it hit scalding geothermal water. The water has been flowing ever since. Over the decades, minerals in it built up into strange, multicolored cones, streaked red, orange and green, that are still growing today.

The catch is that Fly Geyser sits on private land, a ranch called Fly Ranch. For nearly twenty years, it was closed to the public. In 2016, the ranch was bought by the Burning Man Project, the organization behind the famous festival held in the nearby desert. Today, the only way to see the geyser up close is on a small guided walk. And some of its pools are close to boiling.

=== 03_niihau ===
Number eight. Niihau, Hawaii.

Just 17 miles off the coast of Kauai lies a Hawaiian island that most people will never set foot on. In 1864, Elizabeth Sinclair bought Niihau from King Kamehameha the Fifth for $10,000 in gold. Her descendants, the Robinson family, still own it today, and they decide who comes ashore.

About 70 people live on Niihau, and they speak Hawaiian as their first language, every day. The island earned its nickname, the Forbidden Isle, because for generations outsiders were kept away, to protect the people living there from disease and outside influence. Today, a few tightly controlled helicopter tours, hunting trips and dives are allowed. Everything else is up to the family that has owned the island for more than 160 years.

=== 04_surtsey ===
Number seven. Surtsey, Iceland.

In November 1963, an eruption broke the surface of the Atlantic Ocean off the south coast of Iceland. A day later, a ridge of new rock stood 40 meters high. By the time the eruptions ended, Earth had a brand-new island. Iceland named it Surtsey, after Surtr, a fire giant from Norse mythology.

Scientists realized they had been handed something priceless: a chance to watch life arrive on bare, sterile land, from the very beginning. So in 1965, Iceland put the island under protection, and it has been off-limits ever since to everyone except approved researchers. Even they must be careful not to bring a single seed. Moulds and bacteria came first. The first plant appeared in 1965. Birds carried in more seeds. Today, seals breed on Surtsey's beaches, and nearly 90 species of birds have been recorded there. But the sea is slowly wearing the island away.

=== 05_lascaux ===
Number six. Lascaux Cave, France.

In 1940, a group of teenagers in southwestern France followed their dog into a hole in the ground. Inside, they found walls covered with paintings of horses, bulls and deer, around 17,000 years old. It was one of the greatest discoveries of prehistoric art ever made.

Lascaux opened to the public in 1948, and the crowds came. By the early 1960s, more than 1,500 people a day were squeezing into the cave. The heat, the humidity and the microbes they brought with them began to harm the paintings, and green mould spread across the walls. In 1963, Lascaux was closed to the public, and it has stayed closed ever since. Only researchers and conservators may go inside, in sterile overalls and gloves. Everyone else visits a full-size replica nearby.

=== 06_ise ===
Number five. Ise Grand Shrine, Japan.

Deep in a forest in central Japan stands the most sacred shrine of the Shinto religion. Its inner shrine, called Naiku, is dedicated to Amaterasu, the sun goddess. Millions of pilgrims come here every year. But the heart of the shrine is off-limits. Ordinary visitors stop at the outer fences. Beyond them, only priests and the imperial family may go.

And here's what makes Ise unlike anywhere else on Earth: every 20 years, the shrine is rebuilt from scratch. All 125 buildings of the complex are recreated from fresh cypress wood, exactly as before, on a plot right next to the old one. The tradition is about 1,300 years old, with the first recorded rebuilding in the year 690. The current rebuilding is already underway, and in 2033 the goddess will be moved into her new home. Then the cycle begins again.

=== 07_snake_island ===
Number four. Ilha da Queimada Grande, Brazil.

About 35 kilometers off the coast of São Paulo state sits a lush green island with rocky shores and clear blue water. It looks like paradise. Brazilians call it Snake Island.

It is the only home of the golden lancehead, a pit viper found nowhere else on Earth. Cut off from the mainland for around 11,000 years, the snakes learned to hunt the birds that stop on the island. Their venom is said to be up to five times more potent than that of their mainland relatives. Scientists estimate there are a few thousand of them, on an island of just 43 hectares. The golden lancehead is critically endangered, and Brazil has banned visits. Only the navy and a handful of vetted scientists are allowed to land. Everyone else has to admire paradise from a boat.

=== 08_naica ===
Number three. The Cave of the Crystals, Mexico.

In 2000, two brothers drilling a tunnel 300 meters underground in the Naica mine, in northern Mexico, broke into a hidden chamber. Inside were crystals so big it looked like a set from a fantasy film. Giant beams of gypsum, some around 11 meters long, crisscrossed the cave from wall to wall. One geologist called it the Sistine Chapel of crystals.

They grew slowly, over hundreds of thousands of years, in hot, mineral-rich water that once filled the cave. And the cave is still brutally hot: up to 58 degrees Celsius, with humidity above 90 percent. Without protection, it's dangerous to stay longer than about ten minutes, so scientists worked in cooling suits, in short shifts. Then, in 2015, the mine shut down, the pumps were switched off, and the water returned. Today, one of the most beautiful caves on Earth is flooded again, and no one can go in.

=== 09_kailash ===
Number two. Mount Kailash, Tibet.

In the far west of Tibet rises a mountain shaped almost like a pyramid, its faces striped with snow. Mount Kailash is 6,638 meters tall, more than two kilometers lower than Everest. And yet, while thousands of people have stood on top of Everest, nobody has ever climbed Kailash.

The reason isn't difficulty. It's faith. Kailash is sacred to four religions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Bön. Hindus believe it is the home of Shiva. For Buddhists, it is linked to Mount Meru, the center of the universe. More than a billion people consider it holy, and climbing it would be seen as sacrilege, so climbing permits are not granted. Instead, pilgrims walk around the mountain, a journey of about three days. Some cover the whole route by bowing down to the ground with every few steps.

=== 10_qin ===
Number one. The Tomb of Qin Shi Huang, China.

In 1974, farmers digging a well near the city of Xi'an hit fragments of clay. They had found the Terracotta Army: an underground force of at least 8,000 figures, made to guard the first emperor of China, Qin Shi Huang, after his death in 210 BC.

But the soldiers are only the guards. The emperor himself lies under a huge mound nearby, in a burial chamber that has never been opened. More than 2,000 years ago, the historian Sima Qian wrote that 700,000 workers built the tomb, and that mercury was used to create its hundred rivers, including the Yellow River and the Yangtze. It sounded like legend. But when scientists tested the soil above the chamber, they found far more mercury than in the ground around it. China has no plans to open the tomb. Any breach could let in air and water and destroy what's inside. So the greatest tomb in China stays sealed, and no one alive has ever seen it.
